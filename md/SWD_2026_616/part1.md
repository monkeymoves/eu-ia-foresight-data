1. INTRODUCTION: POLITICAL AND LEGAL CONTEXT 
The European Commission 2025 Foresight1 report points out that climate change is a risk 
multiplier that should be addressed, and that the green transition for many countries can result 
in more independence and growth. Particularly for Europe, a successful transition to 
sustainable growth provides the opportunity to simultaneously enhance the Union’s strategic 
autonomy. In the same vein, the Draghi Report2 points out that “if Europe’s ambitious climate 
targets are matched by a coherent plan to achieve them, decarbonisation will be an opportunity 
for Europe. But if we fail to coordinate our policies, there is a risk that decarbonisation could 
run contrary to competitiveness and growth.” 
The EU Emissions Trading System (EU ETS) is the cornerstone of EU climate policy. 
Directive 2003/87/EC (the ‘ETS Directive’) lays down the rules for greenhouse gas emission 
allowance trading within the European Union. The Market Stability Reserve (MSR) for the EU 
Emissions Trading System (EU ETS) was established by Decision (EU) 2015/1814 (the ‘MSR 
Decision’).

The 2040 target includes a possibility to use a limited quantity of high-quality international 
credits to make an ‘adequate contribution' towards the 2040 target, starting in 2036. This 
amount can be up to 5% of 1990 EU net GHG emissions, corresponding to a domestic 
reduction of net GHG emissions of at least by 85% compared to 1990 levels by 2040. A pilot 
period in 2031-2035 may be considered. 
International credits should be integrated to the future climate legislation in a way which 
ensures reaching the climate target cost-efficiently using high-quality and integrity credits. 
High credit integrity must be ensured taking the rules developed under Article 6.4 of the Paris 
Agreement as a baseline. The specific role and deployment of international credits will be based 
on a thorough impact assessment and subject to the development of Union law setting robust 
and high integrity criteria and other safeguards.  
Nevertheless, this impact assessment concludes that a direct integration of international credits 
in the ETS is not appropriate. The EU has had experience of direct integration of Certified 
Emission Reductions (CERs) from the Clean Development Mechanism (CDM) into the EU 
ETS and discontinued this approach due to negative impacts on the effectiveness and integrity 
of the EU ETS. Repeating these results should be avoided. By contrast, adjusting the ETS cap 
equivalent to the amount of international credits that would be centrally purchased from 
mitigation outside of the EU under strict criteria and oversight, is a simpler, straightforward 
approach that aligns with the fundamental principles of the system, ensuring a clearly defined 
cap and environmental integrity, while still granting additional emission space to the EU ETS. 
Revenues associated with the additional allowances that can be auctioned could be earmarked 
to finance the international credits that justify the additional emissions space in the EU ETS. 
This impact assessment therefore uses (modelled) scenarios aiming at an economy-wide

impacts of the Climate Law, in particular on EU ETS sectors. It could be decided later that less

action. Consequently, the domestic reduction of net GHG emissions could be higher than 85%.

GHG emissions from waste treatment are not subject to carbon pricing in most Member States.

waste incineration with energy recovery (waste-to-energy), which is estimated to account for a

In addition, the EU ETS’s contribution to the necessary emissions reductions may be hampered 
by market inefficiencies resulting from a decreasing market size, which is intrinsic to the 
system design. The MSR rules under the ETS aim to tackle structural supply-demand 
imbalances between the available allowances to emit CO2 and the emissions of the compliance 
entities covered. The impact assessment therefore analyses whether the rules governing the 
operation of the MSR remain appropriate in the context of a declining supply of allowances. 
And more precisely, this involves: (i) ensuring that the market remains efficient and creates an 
incentive to invest in decarbonisation in the global context; and (ii) addressing specific 
challenges faced by covered sectors to facilitate the uptake of available solutions. This 
assessment is carried out in consideration of other legislative acts that facilitate the clean 
transition and the competitiveness of EU industry. The impact assessment also examines how 
to optimise the use of ETS revenues in support of decarbonisation investments.

2. PROBLEM DEFINITION 
2.1. 
What are the problems? 
Problem 1: Risk of not contributing cost-effectively (i) to the EU emissions reduction 
target in line with climate neutrality and the 2040 target, and (ii) to addressing the EU 
ETS’s fair share of international emissions 
The overarching objective of the EU ETS is to contribute to reduce GHG emissions cost-
effectively through-out the EU economy, including its fair share of international emissions, in 
a way that is in line with the climate neutrality objective by 2050 and the intermediate 2040 
climate target. However, there is a risk that the EU ETS is not contributing to these emissions 
reductions cost-effectively for several reasons: 
o The current legislative framework was designed in view of the 2030 climate target, so 
the EU ETS cap trajectory is not aligned with the overall climate policy framework that 
ensures a balanced and cost-effective contribution of all sectors to greenhouse gas 
emission reductions and carbon removals towards the 2040 target. 
o As the cap is reduced towards climate neutrality, the carbon price is expected to rise. In 
a market which functions perfectly otherwise, this expectation would be sufficient to 
guarantee long term investment. However, there are other market failures, such as 
insufficient incentive for investing in R&D&I as a public good, or the more myopic 
perspective of investors. Therefore, other risks remain: 
o Insufficient long-term investments will be made to decarbonise certain sectors, 
among other things, due to lack of sufficiently strong carbon price signal as 
perceived by investors. This risk is confirmed by the findings of the evaluation 
(see Annex 15). While the EU ETS has delivered significant emissions 
reductions in the power sector, the relatively low carbon prices, and high share 
of free allocation to industry reduced the incentives to invest in decarbonisation 
technologies.  
o Lack of funding mechanisms of sufficient duration and magnitude to support 
the business case for industrial decarbonisations and thus investments17. In this 
case, ETS risks leading to GHG emissions reductions mainly through a decrease

o The EU ETS risks not contributing cost-effectively to the reduction of the EU’s fair

between the ETS and global measures, for example in case transport is subject to double 
carbon pricing.  
Problem 2: Risk of market inefficiencies  
The EU ETS is designed as a cap-and-trade system, with the cap limiting the total amount of 
GHG that can be emitted by installations and operators covered under the scope of the system. 
As the price of allowances is determined by the market at the intersection of demand and a pre-
determined supply, the European carbon market can be exposed to a certain risk of market 
inefficiencies (e.g. trading frictions, price volatility) as it becomes smaller.

3. WHY SHOULD THE EU ACT? 
3.1. 
Legal basis 
Articles 191, 192 and 193 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union empower 
the EU to act to preserve, protect, and improve the quality of the environment; protect human 
health; and promote measures at the international level to deal with regional or worldwide 
environmental problems. The legal basis of this impact assessment is in Article 192(1), as this 
impact assessment is action being taken to combat climate change and to serve the other 
environmental objectives specified in Article 191. The ETS has been operating on this legal 
basis since 2003. The European Parliament and Council adopted all amendments to the ETS 
Directive on this legal basis.

3.2. 
Subsidiarity: Necessity of EU action 
Climate change is a trans-boundary problem and both international and EU action can

upward convergence. Furthermore, many of the policy elements assessed in this impact

the carbon leakage protection and the low-carbon funding mechanisms. EU action can also 
inspire and promote the development of other market-based measures, including at global level. 
3.3. 
Subsidiarity: Added value of EU action 
As a carbon market, the ETS incentivises emission reductions made by cost-effective solutions 
first across the activities it covers, achieving greater efficiency by virtue of its scale. 
Implementing a similar measure nationally would result in smaller, fragmented carbon markets, 
risking distortions of competition and likely lead to higher overall abatement costs. The same 
logic holds for the extension of carbon pricing to new sectors.

5.2. 
Policy options 
Three policy options have been developed that ensure that the ETS contributes cost-effectively 
to the EU emissions reduction target in line with climate neutrality and the 2040 target and to 
addressing the EU’s fair share of international emissions. The options differ in the degree of 
change that they entail to the current system. Policy option 1 Aligning ambition and improving 
incentives includes the measures that, overall, lead to least change compared to policies in place 
in 2026. Policy option 2 Aligning ambition and extending scope and corresponding incentives 
includes measures that introduce moderate innovations or changes to the system. Policy option 
3 Aligning ambition and extending scope and incentives comprehensively alters most 
substantially how the system works. Annex 7 explains why each measure was included in the 
relevant policy option. In addition, the concluding sections of Annexes 8 to 13 summarise the 
assessment of each measure. Not all the measures analysed for each topic in the annexes were 
included in the policy options. 
The most impactful measures in the policy options are those related to scope (aviation and 
waste), ambition (cap, removals and MSR), and use of allowances (in particular, for the IDB 
and free allocation), impacting for example costs of production or the business case for 
investments in emissions reductions. Regarding the scope, the combined extension of the 
system to departing flights and all waste incineration installations and landfills of non-
hazardous waste (PO3 – largest scope extension) could increase the size of the ETS by around 
30% compared to PO1 in terms of cumulative budget over 2031-2040. 
Regarding the ambition, all policy options include the same measure for the cap, meaning that 
the impact is driven by the integration of carbon removals and the MSR. This choice has been 
made for the pragmatic reason that the other measures, not related to the cap, need to be 
assessed without this assessment being affected by changes in overall ambition level. The cap 
section of Annex 8 and chapter 6 of this impact assessment will nevertheless show 
environmental and economic impacts of comparing CAP2 and CAP4 to CAP3.Various other 
caps and targets are also illustrated in the Annex. The relative impacts of the other measures, 
such as those on removals, carbon leakage or CCU, will not be affected by changes in cap 
trajectory: if a policy measure performs better under certain criteria than an alternative measure 
under CAP1, it can be expected to perform better under CAP3 as well. By keeping the same

5.3. 
Options discarded at an early stage 
Several measures were discarded to address the specific problems, as set out in the description 
the policy options with reference to Annexes 8 to 13. In addition, alternative options that could 
address the problem as a whole by replacing the ETS for another instrument, either harmonised 
at EU-level or for Member State implementation, instead of adjusting the EU ETS through the 
measures examined in the Annexes 8 to 13, were discarded at an early stage. 
Beyond the theoretical advantages of the EU ETS over its alternative policies (e.g. carbon taxes 
or command-and-control measures), the discontinuation or narrowing of the EU ETS would 
lead to economic losses, weaken low carbon investments signals, penalise early movers, reduce 
investor confidence, distort competition, reduce revenues, reduce employment in affected 
firms, and jeopardise climate-related jobs in sectors. These sectors include clean tech, 
renewable energy, energy efficiency, and also monitoring and verification, consulting, and 
innovation support. At an international level, dismantling the EU ETS would undermine the 
EU’s credibility in international climate diplomacy and weaken the EU’s position on shaping 
global carbon pricing mechanisms and border adjustment instruments (see Evaluation Report 
in Annex 15, Section 6.3). Final impacts on the economy and on the credibility of the EU would 
depend on whether the EU ETS is replaced by another policy, by which, and the transition 
period for the new system to function. Feedback from the stakeholder consultation broadly 
confirms the findings. Respondents most frequently emphasised the EU’s added value in 
harmonising implementation across Member States, avoiding a fragmented patchwork of 

<!-- page:27 -->
 
22 
national schemes31 and ensuring a common price signal. Similarly for maritime, 
discontinuation of EU ETS maritime would put at risk the fair contribution of the maritime 
sector to the EU’s climate objectives and undermine the shipping companies that have already 
invested in cleaner vessels and fuels. 
Beyond alternatives to the EU ETS as a whole, in relation to specific measures to modify the 
system, the implications of lowering the 20 MW threshold (the thermal capacity below which 
stationary installations are exempt from EU ETS) is considered in detail in Annex 12 of this 
Impact Assessment. Based on that assessment, the option of lowering the threshold is not taken 
further. Most emissions from small installations will be covered under EU ETS2, which will 
become fully operational in 2028 and is outside the scope of this impact assessment. Therefore, 
the environmental gains from lowering the threshold would be very small compared to the 
substantial increase in administrative effort. 
As regards the Market Stability Reserve, the evaluation and impact assessment demonstrate 
that the MSR should remain a rule-based and transparent framework for supply adjustments in 
the EU ETS, able to respond to external shocks without discretionary and price-based 
interventions or political interference. Alternative far-reaching reforms such as a carbon central 
bank are therefore discarded as they represent departures from the MSR's established 
operational principles, would require multi-year preparatory timelines and hence are not 
feasible for implementation by 2028.

• Net and gross emissions 
The policy options in this impact assessment integrate removals as a way to provide additional 
emissions space. The policy options should ensure the generation of all the removals that are 
taken into account in the design of the cap:  
1. for ETS to achieve its net objective and  
2. for the ETS sectors to benefit from the emissions space that is represented by the 
relevant removals (i.e. the gross objective).

amount of eligible removals32 modelled for the 85% scenario33 of between 175 and 330 Mt.

than projected, there would be a risk of not attaining the net emissions target.

removals as a result thereof cannot be ruled out.

6.1.1.3. MSR 
Changes to the MSR alter the allowance supply over the assessment window via releases, 
providing more liquidity to the market and putting downward pressure on the carbon price. 
Depending on how this affects the timing of surrendering of allowances, this may in turn affect 
cumulative emissions. However, since any MSR release remains within the cap, the system’s 
overall ambition is unchanged. The MSR measures are compared with a baseline that assumes 
no further changes to either EU ETS or the MSR.  
Policy option PO1 is based on the MSR1 measure as described in Annex 8. Under this measure, 
parameters of the MSR design are then kept constant over the time until 2040.  
Policy options PO2 and PO3 on the other hand, are based on the MSR3 measure, which 
assumes parameters decline at a fixed proportional rate from 2028 onwards. This ensures that 
all parameters under PO2 and PO3 are adapted on an annual basis to take account of the overall 
decreasing market of allowances.

For the IDB, the assessment in terms of environmental and other impacts to be achieved is 
based on assessment of the Baseline size of the IDB (EUR 100 billion) which is defined in the

support formats and geographical balance would only have marginal bearing on the impacts

premiums should be more cost-efficient, thus resulting in higher emissions savings, stronger 
private capital mobilisation and larger spillover benefits, although results will critically depend

all of them. In addition, the trade-off has to be considered between cost-efficiency (delivered

delivered by administrative schemes with fixed premia that could allow a more robust financial 
model for projects, increase likelihood of projects materialising and better diversification of 
portfolio). In general, the mechanisms that bring high level of competition are better suited to 
more mature technologies that operate on deep and liquid markets, which should progressively 
become the case for many decarbonisation technologies. Administrative schemes should be 
needed in the early years of the IDB operation when uncertainties are the highest and the 
business case the most difficult.

activity levels. The reforms to free allocation considered in this assessment continues in this 
direction, aiming to ensure a fair balance between safeguarding current competitiveness of 
European industry and encouraging emissions reductions and investments to promote long term 
competitiveness. The following reforms to free allocation are reflected in the policy options: 
• In PO1, free allocation is reformed in accordance with the CL1 measure, which assumes 
a continuation of free allocation for non-CBAM sectors based on the existing rules, but 
with some streamlining and simplification.  
• In PO2, free allocation is reformed in accordance with the CL2 measure, which assumes 
that a progressive share of free allocation is conditional on investments with two 
variants of compliance modelled (i.e. 90% compliance and 50 % compliance). This 
policy options also includes a revision of the CBAM phase-out factor.  
• In PO3, free allocation is reformed in accordance with the CL3 measure, which assumes 
that free allowances are phased out for non-CBAM sectors at the same rate as for 
CBAM sectors so that from 2034 onwards no further free allocation is provided to 
industry. 
In addition, scenario variants for PO1 and PO3 marked with R include revised revenue 
recycling assumptions where analysed, while scenario variants marked with C for PO2 refer to 
variants with a slow-down of the phase-out of free allocation for the sectors under the scope of 
CBAM. A more detailed description of the scenarios used in the GEM-E3 analysis can be found 
in Annex 12, Section 1.4.1.  
These reforms to the provision of free allocation that are included within the policy options are 
compared to the baseline scenario, which considers a phase out of free allocation until 2040. 
The conventional approach for carbon leakage assessment considers the emissions that increase 
in one (not regulated) region whilst decreasing in another regulated region and can be expressed 
by the following simple formula: Carbon leakage rate = increase in foreign emissions / 
decrease in domestic emissions. However, EU27 EU ETS GHG emissions increase from 
baseline levels in all policy options, as the ambition level is reduced. Within the limitations of 
the GEM-E3 model, the results show that as these emissions increase, the ETS sectors covered 
are not threatened by carbon leakage risks. Instead, the impacts on EU, non-EU and global 
GHG emissions are presented to discuss environmental implications both at the EU level and 
beyond. 
Emissions are influenced both by the number of free allowances allocated and by the carbon 
price required to meet the EU ETS cap. In PO2—and especially in PO3—the inclusion of waste 
emissions in EU ETS results in an increase of the cap, which leads to higher emissions across 
all EU ETS sectors. In addition, the increase of the cap for PO1 and PO2 result in higher

Indirect carbon cost (ICC) compensation can provide substantial environmental benefits, since 
electrified technologies have the potential to provide up to 90% of Europe’s industrial heat 
demand by 2035. Electrification, combined with decarbonisation of electricity supply, 
therefore delivers a climate benefit. The extent of the benefit depends on the mixture of fossil-
based industrial processes that are replaced, as well as the emissions from increased electricity 
demand. ICC compensation supports the business case for electro-intensive industrial 
processes by reducing their carbon costs. It is particularly relevant for electrified industrial 
processes that face an indirect carbon cost and compete with non-electrified processes that emit 
CO2 directly and benefit from free allocation. Conceptually, the environmental benefit of ICC 
compensation is therefore a combination of: 
• Carbon leakage protection: addressing the risk of electro-intensive industries 
relocating, due to indirect carbon costs, to areas with less strict climate governance. 
• Incentivising electrification: supporting the business case for electrification relative to 
direct emissions, for industries that could electrify their processes.  
Under continuation of current policies, the ETS Directive would continue to encourage 
Member States to provide ICC compensation. Article 10a(6) would continue to apply, outlining 
the key features of Member States’ compensation measures (e.g. they should be based on 
indirect emission benchmarks corresponding to the most efficient available technologies and 
emissions of the relevant European electricity production mix). Future State aid rules (which 
are not the subject of this Impact Assessment) would determine more detailed parameters such 
as sectoral eligibility and any other conditions that beneficiaries would have to meet.  
Policy option ICC1 would continue the State-aid-based approach and propose limited 
amendments to the ETS Directive’s ICC provisions.  
In policy option ICC2, an ICC Fund would be established to provide compensation at an EU-
level on a harmonised basis in order to reduce distortion of the single market and provide a 
level playing field for industry. Due to competing claims on ETS auction revenues (see Section 
6.1.1.4) the proposed scale of the fund (e.g. 5% of the average revenue for 2031-2040) would 
be smaller than the potential scale of ICC compensation if all Member States were to provide 
State aid post-2030 using schemes similar to those currently in place post-2030 (at least 11%, 
see Annex 12 for analysis of ICC drivers). As a result, Member States would retain the option 
to provide ICC compensation as State aid, up to the level permitted in the guidelines.  
The options’ potential environmental benefits would be similar. However, the benefit of ICC2 
could be slightly larger since some beneficiaries from an EU fund may not otherwise have 
received State aid under ICC1.

6.1.1.8. Waste 
All sectors in the economy need to decarbonise to achieve climate neutrality in 2050. Under 
the baseline, emissions from waste incineration and landfills would continue to be mainly 
covered by the Effort Sharing Regulation until 2030 and are not driven by a carbon price, with 
post-2030 coverage subject to future review. Emissions from municipal waste incineration are 
expected to continue increasing at least into the early 2030s in line with recent trends, planned 
capacity increases and absent additional abatement incentives, while emissions from landfilling 
are projected to continue to gradually decrease, albeit slower than stipulated due to lagging 
implementation of the current regulatory framework and remaining responsible for the largest 
share of waste sector emissions. 
Extending the scope of the EU ETS to activities in the waste management sector would increase 
accountability for emissions in this sector – representing up to 3% of EU GHG emissions 
depending on the scope of the policy options considered – and provide for increased certainty 
in delivering GHG emissions reductions, since the cap sets a limit on the emissions that 
economic operators can account for by surrendering allowances. Such certainty is not possible 
through other types of measures. 
Compared to the continuation of current policies, policy option PO1 would not substantially 
improve the environmental outcome, only implementing legal clarifications to improve 
certainty on the current scope. 
Policy option PO2 would extend carbon pricing to municipal waste incineration (MWI), 
driving the largest decrease in cumulative emissions from waste incineration, of -53 MtCO2eq 
or -22% over the period 2031-2040 compared to the baseline, with most of the decrease 
projected to occur in the second half of the decade. The carbon price would provide an 
economic incentive to reduce emissions cost-effectively at different stages of the waste value 
chain, through a combination of improved waste prevention, reduction and pre-collection 
sorting at source and improved post-collection mixed-waste sorting and recycling in the 
shorter-term, and carbon capture for residual emissions in the medium- to longer-term (as of 
mid-decade). Both increased recycling and carbon capture provide dual climate benefits in the 
waste management sector. Increased recycling reduces GHG emissions from waste disposal 
and generates recycled materials displacing more carbon-intensive virgin materials, while 
carbon capture reduces fossil emissions in combination with generating biogenic carbon 
removals. Similar to other hard-to-abate sectors, targeted policy and investment support can 
advance the business case for carbon capture and strengthen planning and investment 
predictability. Complementary waste policies under the future Circular Economy Act will 
contribute to ensure a comprehensive approach by stimulating the single market and demand 
for secondary materials and circular products, supporting investment in recycling capacity, 
reducing the landfilling and incineration of used raw materials, and rolling-out upstream 
producer-oriented policies such as eco-design requirements and strengthened Extended 
Producer Responsibility (see Annex 13, Section 1.3 for a detailed assessment of how the carbon 
price can drive behavioural changes, costs and opportunities contributing to emissions 
reductions).  The inclusion of municipal waste incineration installations under PO2 and PO3 
will also support the efforts to promote a more circular economy via the use of captured CO2 
as a feedstock through a hybrid downstream accounting for non-permanent CCU products (see 
Section 6.1.1.9.)  by accounting for the downstream emissions associated with non-permanent 
CCU products. 
In policy option PO3, the scope of the EU ETS would also be extended to CO2-emissions from 
hazardous waste incineration (HWI) and to methane emissions from non-hazardous landfilling,

of emissions leakage to landfills is contained by the zero-rating of biogenic emissions in 
incineration, limiting the incentive to divert organic waste to landfills – the main source of 
methane emissions – to circumvent the carbon cost. PO3, by including methane emissions 
reductions in landfills, would address any residual risks of organic waste diversion from 
incineration to landfills. Emissions leakage risks to hazardous waste incineration are limited 
by technical and operational constraints, higher costs, and strict regulatory requirements under 
PO2 and would be absent under PO3. Finally, the risk of emissions leakage to third countries 
is limited by the strict regulatory framework on waste exports in place (Waste Shipment 
Regulation – see Annex 13, Section 1.5).

end-of-life treatment, such as recycling or landfilling, they would not be altered chemically and 
the CO2 embedded in them is likely not to be released throughout the waste management 
process.  
CO2 used to produce e-fuels will also not represent a loophole risk, as their combustion will 
mostly occur within the scope of the EU ETS, in sectors such as maritime and aviation. E-fuels 
are also subject to specific certification requirements and are easily tracked across the value 
chain. However, to avoid undercounting of emissions used to produce e-fuels would 
necessarily mean that it is not possible anymore to zero-rate the emissions from the combustion 
of these fuels.

products or applications, the risk can be best addressed by applying the upstream accounting 
as a derogation to a general rule or setting additional requirements or limitations to the use of 
such products to minimise the risks. Nevertheless, addressing all possible loopholes risks is not 
feasible in practice without severely restricting the use of non-permanent CCU applications, 
and therefore limiting their development and foregoing the benefits they bring. Given the low 
volumes of CCU products currently being produced, these risks are only hypothetical. 
Therefore, it is preferable to adopt an approach that regularly assesses the effective risk posed 
by non-permanent CCU to the integrity of the ETS and introduces corrective mechanisms if 
and when such risks start having a material impact. For a more detailed analysis of the risks of 
undercounting emissions, see Annex 13.

6.1.2.1. Removals 
Broadly speaking, the more effective a measure is at incentivizing CDR deployment (see 
Section 6.1.1.2), the higher the effect of additional biomass use is expected to be. In PO2 and 
PO3, the operator integration routes would likely see little effect on additional biomass use 
during the 2031-2040 period, except if further CDR policy support is put in place. PO3 would 
have the additional effect of the reduced scarcity in the system by setting a gross cap with 1-
in-1-out approach, making technologies such as BioCCS even less competitive. In the presence 
of CDR support outside the ETS however, these measures would see an increase in biomass 
demand in line with biomass eligibility criteria and the relative cost competitiveness across 
CDR technologies, bounded by the CDR limit. PO1 would provide more predictable support 
through the public purchasing programme, and thus affect biomass use accordingly. However, 
the primary levers in addressing risks from biomass over-reliance lie not in measure selection, 
but primarily on the establishment of strict sustainability criteria, accounting for indirect land-
use change, and effectively internalizing externalities from biomass use, which are under 
different regulatory frameworks. Under the CRCF operators generating carbon removals need 
to comply with the do no significant harm principle and additional specific requirements related 
to the type of carbon removal activity they are operating (see Annex 8 – Part II, Section 3.1.2.4). 
Additional sustainability criteria beyond the CRCF methodologies are not considered for the 
removal units to be included in the ETS. However, in a wider context, the incentive structure 
for biomass should be reviewed. NGO’s OPC responses highlighted this as a general concern 
regarding the integration of carbon removals in the EU ETS. 
6.1.2.2. Instruments to support investments 
The IDB is expected to generate broader environmental co-benefits beyond greenhouse gas 
emission reductions, including lower air pollutant emissions (such as NOx, SOx and particulate 
matter), reduced resource and water use, and less waste generation. It can also improve the 
environmental performance of industrial sites and support more circular production models, 
while potential local trade-offs are managed through existing environmental safeguards and 
permitting. However, the differences among IDB options in terms of allocation method, 
support formats and geographical balance would only have marginal differences.  
Under the current ETS Directive, the Modernisation Fund is instrumental in phasing out solid 
and liquid fossil fuels in Member States which are heavily reliant on them for the provision of 
electricity and heating. Under the baseline scenario (discontinuation of the MF in 2030), 
investments available to such activities can be expected to diminish.  
Under all considered policy options, the Modernisation Fund would continue to support 
beneficiary Member States in reducing their dependency on fossil fuels, with an even greater 
impact on the reduction of liquid fossil fuels, which would be either fully excluded or strictly 
limited. All options also reduce support to waste-to-energy. 
Phasing out fossil fuels and support to waste-to-energy has positive co-benefits for health and 
the environment through increased air quality and reduced air pollution control costs.

6.1.2.5. Waste 
Action is needed at different levels to move waste up the waste hierarchy. Many Member States 
are currently not on track to achieve their 2025 and 2030 recycling and recovery targets under 
the EU’s environmental policy framework.  
Extending carbon pricing to GHG emissions from municipal waste incineration (PO2) can 
provide an important complementary financial incentive supporting the circular economy by 
making waste prevention, recycling and reuse more financially competitive through the 
application of a carbon price on waste management processes further down the waste hierarchy. 
PO2 is additionally projected to lead to improved air quality for citizens, as increased waste 
reduction, prevention and recycling incentivised by the carbon price signal reduces harmful 
emissions by reducing or diverting materials like plastics and organics from incineration, 
thereby lowering the formation of air pollutants and toxic compounds. Member States with 
high recycling rates are overall found to have lower environmental life cycle costs than those 
with high disposal rates. In isolation, PO2 carries a risk of waste, in particular fossil waste 
streams, being diverted from incineration to landfills where no effective regulatory or fiscal 
policies (reduction targets, taxation, bans on organic and combustible waste streams sent to 
landfilling) to discourage landfilling are in place. In those cases, higher compliance and 
operational costs for municipal waste incineration may further increase the cost differential 
with landfilling, incentivising some operators or waste producers to look for cheaper treatment 
or disposal routes, which would run counter to the waste hierarchy. This was also consistently 
raised in the public consultation, with a majority of respondents stressing the need to address 
potential risks of diversion to landfills.  
The EU has a comprehensive framework to reduce landfilling in place. However, 
implementation varies significantly across Member States and national regulatory and fiscal 
measures differ widely in scope, enforcement and impact. In addition to PO2, the harmonised

whether within-year or inter-annual, tends to occur in markets characterised by low liquidity. 
Nonetheless, the inter-annual variability of the price paths shown reveals information about the 
comparative stability offered by the different policy options. As explained above, PO1 exhibits 
a notable price dip around 2030-2034, followed by a rapid and steep increase through to 2040, 
which would prove challenging for regulated entities facing such a price trajectory. In contrast, 
PO2 and PO3 demonstrates first a stabilisation of prices in the period of 2030-2034, followed 
by a progressive, monotonic price trajectory until 2040, offering a credible and steady price 
signal that facilitates long-term investment planning. 
Market liquidity 
The TNAC-to-emissions ratio is used as an approximate measure of market liquidity. In all 
policy options, the ratio decreases from its high historical values to about 0.6 for the 2027 
TNAC, given the downward TNAC technical adjustment for the historic net aviation demand 
in 2012-2023 of approximately 173 million EUAs. The ratio then slightly decreases by 2030, 
after which it diverges in the different policy options.  
In PO1, the decrease continues slowly until 2036 to 0.29, a year after the first MSR release 
takes place. After that, due to the continued annual releases, the ratio rises again and reaches 
its maximum value of 0.68 in 2040, exhibiting a u-shaped pattern. 
Starting in 2027, the PO2 & PO3 options include extra-EU aviation one-way emissions, not 
included in PO1, explaining the slight translation between their respective trajectories after 
2027 (before other factors make them diverge further). Likewise, the inclusion of hazardous 
waste and landfills emissions from 2031 in PO3, not covered in PO2, creates the translation 
between their respective curves, otherwise comparable in evolution. 
For PO2 & PO3, the ratio starts to increase again in 2038, due to the first MSR releases, and 
then oscillates in a narrow band, maintained by ongoing releases by the MSR, suggesting a 
steady state for this indicator. The spread of the ratio for the 2027-2040 period is slightly lower 
for PO2 (0.28-0.59) than for PO3 (0.25-0.59), although both are within an acceptable range. 
The PO1 spread is wider (0.29-0.68), and features values on the relatively high range in the 
last years of the period (above 0.6).

6.2.1.3. Instruments to support investments  
The IDB by providing the support to investments will help the industry to stay on the net-zero 
trajectory. It thus helps to avoid higher compliance costs for industry stemming from further 
increases in the ETS carbon price that would have happened in case investments do not 
materialise or are delayed.  
Considering the role of the IDB to contribute to ensuring that the investments foreseen in the 
2040 climate target modelling materialises, there should be a limited/no impact on the ETS 
carbon price that was modelled. In the modelling exercise investments happen due to the carbon 
price trajectory and regulatory framework, fully implemented and perfectly integrated in the 
decision-making of all economic actors. In reality, however, there will still be shortcomings 
compared to model-based exercise and those shortcomings need to be addressed by the support 
mechanisms. 
The IDB has no compliance costs for the ETS installation, instead it will turn the ETS resources 
into the investment support for the industry so that the industry can follow the net-zero 
trajectory. The magnitude of this support will vary across the IDB options primarily due to type 
of support (fixed or variable premium) and even more granular support features than those 
analysed in this Impact Assessment, e.g. the size of fixed premium.

6.2.1.8. CCU 
Compared to the baseline, PO1 represents no change to the current upstream accounting, which 
continues to reward only the capture of emissions for permanent storage (CCU0). As CCS is 
not an economically feasible option for all installations that could benefit from carbon capture, 
upstream accounting effectively restricts the development of alternative technologies that make 
use of the capture carbon instead of storing it. Therefore, under PO1, the upstream accounting 
negatively affects the efficiency and effectiveness of the EU ETS in achieving its objectives.  
The measure CCU3, hybrid downstream and midstream accounting, included in PO2 and PO3 
effectively addresses the issues outlined in the previous paragraph and it enables investments

CO2 feedstock and it improves the business case for CCU products. The midstream accounting 
encompassed in measure CCU3 also best addresses the specificities of e-fuels, for which there 
is a well-developed regulatory framework is already in place.  
The hybrid accounting under PO2 and PO3 would incentivise the capture of CO2 and provide 
flexibility for operators to decide whether the CO2 should be stored or utilised in different types

6.2.3.2.Instruments to support investments 
All IDB options effectively create the business case for private investment in industrial 
decarbonisation technologies by covering sufficient share of the funding gap, though the 
outcomes will depend on further, more granular features of funding mechanisms like eligible 
technologies, eligible project stages or compensation structures (not analysed in this Impact 
Assessment).  
Administrative schemes and fixed premia offer clear, simple mechanisms that integrate easily 
into corporate planning, while auctions with variable premia are more cost-efficient driving the 
high level of competition but eliciting also projects willing to take on more risks. There are 
risks of non-delivery (i.e. projects not materialising) inherent to all options and they would 
need to be analysed on more granular level (e.g. setting penalties on non-delivery or overly 
strict criteria triggering underbidding). Those risks are more prominent in IDB option packages 
that put projects under higher level of competition (PO1 and PO3), while on the other hand the 
approach of administrative schemes (in PO2) mitigates those risks, especially if favouring 
projects that are first to deliver on the market.  
In general, the mechanism that bring high level of competition (PO1 and PO3) are better suited 
to more mature technologies that operate on deep and liquid markets, which should 
progressively become the case for many decarbonisation technologies. Administrative schemes 
(PO2) should be needed in the early years of the IDB operation when uncertainties are the 
highest and the business case the most difficult. 
Through the IDB, industrial sectors in particular stand to benefit from accelerated deployment 
of decarbonisation solutions, which can mitigate the competitiveness pressures associated with 
rising carbon prices and reduce the transition risks (i.e. investments delayed or not 
materialising).  
In addition to the direct investments provided by the IDB, the EC analysis indicates that a direct 
investment leverage effect of IDB support could be expected between 5% and 75%, depending 
on the sector and technology, with a weighted average of 45% across all modelled projects.  
It must be emphasised that the industrial decarbonisation requires substantial upfront capital 
investments (CAPEX) in plants with long lifetimes (typically 15-25 years), but it is operating 
costs, particularly energy-related that dominate total production costs, a trend amplified for

GEM-E3 model results provide insights into how alternative ETS design choices translate into 
heterogenous sectoral and macroeconomic impacts, relative to the baseline scenario by taking 
into consideration key drivers within scenario assumptions such as the different free allocation 
distributions across industry, as well as the different ETS coverage and cap stringency and 
alternative revenue recycling. The policy scenarios assessed by the GEM-E3 model are 
described in Section 6.1.1.7 and in Annex 12, Section 1.4.1. 
Changes in the unit cost of production are driven by specific scenario assumptions namely: (i) 
different shares of free allocation, with highest shares in PO2_90, (ii) changes in carbon prices, 
notably in PO2 and PO3 where carbon prices are lower than the reference case due to the 
expansion of EU ETS coverage to waste emissions, (iii) other scenario implications of waste 
inclusion in EU ETS, such as the increase in the price of electricity and (iv) the impact arising 
from revenue recycling. The dominant driver of the sectoral effects varies between these four 
mechanisms across sectors.

the baseline, despite the full phase-out of free allowances after 2034. This improvement in 
competitiveness is driven by the inclusion of waste emissions in EU ETS, which leads on to a 
decline in the carbon price. By contrast, policy options with alternative revenue recycling 
(PO3R and PO1R) exhibit lower competitiveness, as redirecting revenues away from power-
sector investment towards households increases domestic demand, which in turn raises both 
capital and labour costs.

6.2.4.2. Maritime 
The extension of the ETS to smaller vessels under PO2 is expected to primarily affect SMEs, 
since companies managing smaller vessels tend to be on average smaller (i.e. 1.6 vessels per 
company) than those already reporting under the EU maritime MRV (2.6 vessels per company). 
In addition, most companies managing smaller vessels would be new to the MRV and ETS 
system and would need to set up and familiarise themselves with MRV and ETS processes. A 
key disadvantage for SMEs is their more limited capacity to absorb additional administrative 
costs. While some administrative costs borne by shipping companies are dependent on fleet 
size, other costs are fixed. As such, the proportionate administrative burden is likely to be 
higher for smaller businesses who do not benefit from significant economies of scale. SMEs in 
the maritime sector are expected to sell or buy allowances via intermediary traders mainly from 
the financial sector instead of participating directly in the primary or secondary markets. 
Another key disadvantage for SMEs is their limited ability to access financing for capital 
investments in low-carbon technologies (e.g., alternative propulsion systems or onboard carbon 
capture). This constraint may reduce the range of greenhouse gas abatement options available 
to them, potentially increasing their exposure to ETS-related costs in the short term.

6.2.6. Impacts on the functioning of the internal market 
6.2.6.1. Instruments to support investments 
For the IDB, the options on ensuring geographical balance would be those that also have the 
most tangible impact on the internal market. Most likely and at least in the short term, the best 
efficiency and the best outcome for the internal market from the IDB would be achieved 
through the pure EU-level competitions that favours the most mature projects, with the best 
locations, benefiting from the most favourable natural endowment (in terms of renewables 
potentials), industrial structures, infrastructure and ecosystems and supportive national 
policies. 
In the long term, however, this option can lead to missing some opportunities in 
countries/regions that are hampered by lack of administrative capacity/lack of innovation 
ecosystem or infrastructure rather than structural factors of their industry/economy. This option 
would also mean that the leverage and spillover effects due to the IDB is concentrated in some 
of the regions. 
To sum up, strict national allocations (that can also take the form of ‘as a service’ or right of 
the first use – see Annex 9) can be perceived as the least efficient option because they limit the 
benefits of the EU-level competition. In the longer term, however, such approach might help 
to address some structural problems in certain regions (e.g. if funded activities create sufficient 
demand for new infrastructure or lead to creation of new industrial structures).  
The best balance would be most likely achieved by a mix between the EU level competition 
and national allocations as it draws to large extent the benefits of the EU-level competitions 
and yet safeguards part of funding for national projects with all positive leverage and spillover 
effects that it will bring. This approach would avoid, in the longer-term, missing opportunities 
in terms of unlocking the potential of all EU Member States in terms of industrial 
decarbonisation.  
Continuation of the Modernisation Fund will further support lower-income Member States’ 
catching-up process relative to higher income ones in terms of modernisation of energy systems 
and carbon intensity of the economy. Thanks to investments supported by the Fund, beneficiary 
Member States can develop the needed infrastructure for the clean energy transition (e.g. stable 
and modern grids, storage capacity, sizeable renewable energy capacity, EV charging 
infrastructure) and attract more private investments. This is therefore conducive to creating a 
level-playing field among Member States and alleviating some barriers to the single market.

6.2.6.2. Carbon leakage prevention 
For direct emissions, continuation of free allocation is compatible with the internal market, 
since eligibility for free allocation is harmonised at EU-level in all options considered. For 
ICC, Option ICC1 would continue the current policy, which allows Member States to decide 
whether to offer ICC compensation or not. This system results in a somewhat fragmented

The partial harmonisation of ICC compensation under option ICC2 would reduce this 
fragmentation by offering some compensation on a harmonised basis at EU-level. This would 
reduce internal market fragmentation to a limited extent by compensating a fraction of the 
potential indirect cost through a harmonised fund. Funding equivalent to 5% of 2030-2040 
auction revenues is level is suggested based on striking a balance between the benefits of ICC 
harmonisation, compared to other uses of ETS revenues. 
The issue of indirect carbon costs was often raised by industry stakeholders as this is often a 
cost that they have no direct control over. It is addressed to some extent by national decisions 
on whether to offer ICC compensation. However, the question of how to reduce industrial 
electricity prices is broader and depends on national and European efforts to decarbonise 
electricity supply and modernise the electricity grid in a way that also ensures the price of 
electricity does not become excessively high in comparison to industrial competitors.  
The continuation of indirect cost compensation was consistently supported (with 74% of 
respondents in favour) by industrial stakeholders during the open public consultation. Overall, 
harmonisation was supported by 39% of stakeholders who responded to the question of whether 
indirect cost compensation will remain necessary after 2030 to address the risk of carbon 
leakage from indirect carbon costs not covered by other measures (such as CBAM). Several 
respondents in favour of harmonisation, indicated the need to re-visit different sectors’ 
eligibility for ICC compensation, which is outside the scope of this review.

disposal. This unlevel playing field contributes to competitive distortions within the waste 
management sector, between Member States, and between sectors. First, the co-incineration of

signal or instrument for the sector, leading to competitive distortion on the internal market for 
waste. Third, while power generation installations are covered by the EU ETS, waste-to-energy 
installations are generally not, contributing to an unlevel playing field with renewable energy 
production. Extending the EU ETS to municipal waste incineration installations under PO2 
would contribute to harmonise coverage of waste incineration under the EU ETS, ensuring a 
level playing field and addressing competitive distortions within the waste sector, between

7.2. 
Efficiency 
The efficiency of each policy option can be assessed by comparing the expected benefits 
(i.e., reduction of direct costs or externalities) against the respective expected costs incurred 
for the delivery of each the policy options. Therefore, efficiency concerns the extent to which 
objectives can be achieved for a given level of resource/at least cost.  
The combined measures under the different POs have economic, social, and environmental 
impacts. The major costs of the policy options come in the form of an increase in the 
adjustment costs, administrative and indirect costs for the private sector to implement the 
requirements of the policy options. Additionally, EU Member State authorities are expected 
to benefit from an increase in revenues from higher volumes of auctioned allowances and 
this will offset costs associated with the implementation of the reforms.  
Given that all policy options envisage a less stringent ETS cap, the costs and investments 
are expected to be less compared to the baseline. These lower system costs (excluding 
auction payments) in PO1 and PO2 are mainly driven by changes in the cumulative annual 
expenses borne by final energy users, which include operating and capital expenditures as 
well as taxes. 
In PO1 providing certainty and thus lower capital costs for removals will benefit both 
removals producers and consumers. However, it does result in a burden placed on the public 
authority to either deliver on the removals or face non-attainment of the environmental 
objective. If a limit is applied to the removals that can be purchased the full theoretical 
economic benefits would also be limited to a maximum, although the guaranteed availability 
of removals would allow higher gross emissions, as well as lower prices for the removals.  
In PO2, the higher uncertainty for removals producers leads to higher discount rates and 
lower volumes. As a result, abatement efforts expected to be higher, particularly in industry 
(as reflected in higher capital costs relative to the baseline), but the total costs of removals 
are lower, as a result of smaller removal volumes. The higher EU ETS price in such a 
scenario increases auction revenues, which could be recycled into the economy, also to 
support or expand removal activities. This feedback could partially offset the higher EU ETS 
price, though the net effect remains uncertain due to efficiency and transaction cost 
considerations. When including auction payments, system costs would be higher. 
In PO3 modelling results for removals would be driven by the constraint of attaining the 
targets for 2040 and 2050. However, the higher uncertainty for removals and the lack of 
incentive for reducing below the gross emissions cap might well lead to an 
underachievement of the removal objective and therefore an underachievement of the 2040 
target. As this consideration is not modellable, it should be acknowledged qualitatively.  
In sum, the most efficient option for removals would be PO1, as this option gives highest 
certainty to the removals sector allowing it to scale and learn, and as it gives gross emissions 
space to EU ETS operators.  
In addition to the costs and benefits from the cap, waste management integration and carbon 
removals, results have been integrated in this impact assessment through GEM-E3 
modelling that focuses on the macro-economic costs and benefits that focuses on a 
comparison of PO1 to the 85% PRIMES scenario. The impact of changes to free allocation 
envisaged under PO2 and PO3 are reflected and the modelling output does take into account 
the potential change of scope (i.e. inclusion of waste management activities), but not of other 
measures included in policy measures PO1-3.

7.3. 
Coherence 
The ETS is a well-established cornerstone of the EU's policy to combat climate change and 
its key tool for reducing greenhouse gas emissions cost-effectively. With its focus on 
markets and economic emission reduction incentives, it is coherent with other EU policies 
which primarily address non-price barriers. The EU ETS evaluation (see Annex 15) 
highlighted the coherence of the instrument with complementary policies for energy sector, 
such as the Renewable Energy Directive and the Energy Efficiency Directive. Together, 
these instruments were responsible for significative emissions reductions in the energy 
sector.  
Increasing the environmental contribution of the ETS with an enlarged scope, as in PO2 and 
more extensively in PO3, does not change its technology-neutral character, allowing it to 
continue to run alongside sector-specific policies. The Market Stability Reserve will 
continue to enhance policy synergies by mitigating supply/demand imbalances regardless of 
their origin, for instance by reducing the impact that complementary and overlapping 
policies in the area of renewables or energy efficiency can have on the carbon market. PO2 
and PO3 are most coherent for the MSR as they allow to maintain narrower TNAC-to-
emissions ratios through timely releases whilst preserving deeper post-2040 intervention 
capacity. Both options also demonstrate the lowest inter-annual price volatility with smooth

trajectories resilient to shocks and provide for the most optimal market conditions balancing 
responsiveness with transparency. 
For aviation, including departing flights under PO2 and PO3 is coherent with the scope of 
application of ReFuelEU Aviation and automatically unlocks additional ETS-financed 
support for SAF. Both PO2 and PO3 are supportive of multilateral action, as they allow the 
deduction of CORSIA offsetting costs.  
For maritime, all policy options are fully consistent with existing EU legislation such as the 
EU maritime transport MRV framework and FuelEU. The deduction mechanism in PO1 and 
PO2 would ensure coherence with future IMO measures (once adopted) 
The integration of carbon removals in all POs and the recognition of non-permanent CCU

The inclusion of the waste management sector into the EU ETS in PO2 and PO3 is coherent

economy, and the broader environmental policy framework of the EU.  
There are no marked differences between the POs on Industrial Decarbonisation Bank in 
terms of coherence. The IDB as a whole, however, needs to ensure coherence with other

the investments take place. The strengthened EU-level support provided by the continuation 
of the Innovation Fund and new Industrial Decarbonisation Bank are from an EU single

also driving the investments but currently not fully sufficient). Coherence with other EU-
wide funding mechanisms can be fostered by focusing on specific stages of the technology 
development or ‘investment journey’ (avoiding overlaps with other programmes) as well as 
by creating an aligned governance framework of ECF and IDB. The coherence with other 
ETS based support instruments and the national use of ETS revenues continues to be 
fostered, including via State aid rules that enable the funding on the national level. 
Additionally, the set-up of the ‘as-a-service’ feature could enable even better integration of 
the State aid and EU-level funding if applied to the IDB funding instruments.  
Expanding eligibility of priority investments to CCS and direct electrification of industry in 
all POs is coherent with the policies being developed under the Industrial Carbon 
Management Strategy and the broader framework of the Clean Industrial Deal. After 2030, 
hard-to-abate sectors will still need deployment of solutions like CCS and direct 
electrification which are costly. The phase out of public support to investments involving 
natural gas and waste-to-energy is also consistent with the EU’s 2040 decarbonation targets 
since lower-emitting alternatives exist. In that respect, PO3 is the most consistent with the 
EU’s decarbonation targets, followed by PO2 and finally by PO1.

8. PREFERRED OPTION 
8.1. 
Identification of the preferred policy option 
All policy options achieve the same ETS emissions reduction target, although for variable 
scope / share of the economy-wide emission with PO2 and PO3 each adding additional 
scope. The decision on the preferred policy option is informed by their relative effectiveness 
and efficiency. While each PO has been primarily modelled with CAP2, each PO could be 
implemented under CAP3, with the same relative impacts as in above comparison table of 
the policy options relative to the baseline scenario. 
PO2 was considered to be the preferred policy option. Together, its measures strengthen the 
environmental effectiveness of the system while maintaining proportionality, regulatory 
stability, and fairness across sectors, and safeguarding competitiveness. 
Whilst PO3 was in some regards more effective (i.e. in terms of extending the scope further 
and reducing the phase out of free allocation to enhance the price signal), the efficiency of 
this option was lower due to the additional costs in terms of adjustment and administrative 
effort compared to the other policy options. 
PO1 was also deemed to not be the preferred policy option as it did not achieve all the 
objectives that were set for the assessment. For example, this option was not considered to 
be effective in addressing future concerns regarding the functioning of the market as it did 
not increase the size of the market to new activities such as in the waste sector, and it had 
the weakest improvements in terms of enabling investments. It also showed the weakest 
results towards ensuring effectiveness of EU climate action on international transport while 
avoiding double effort. 
The proposed reforms brought by PO2 maintain the existing compliance framework of the 
EU ETS, while introducing new obligations and funding streams, mainly during the 2031-
2040 period, that are considered the preferred options:  
• From 2031, the revised cap (and from 2028 the reformed MSR parameters) would 
apply to all ETS sectors, reducing the decreasing rate of cap, lowering the carbon 
prices and improving market predictability. 
• Free allocation is continued as a carbon leakage prevention mechanism, but part of 
the free allocation is gradually made conditional on decarbonisation investments. 
Additional free allocation is made available to CBAM sectors to address residual 
carbon leakage risks. 
• Public authorities, both at the national and EU level, would extend ETS registries 
and oversight systems to cover new operator categories and activities, and verify 
conditionality compliance for free allocation. These new administrative tasks are 
expected to build on existing processes and to be operational by 2031.

future IMO global measures.  
• The inclusion of all municipal waste incinerators would enter into force from 2031, 
allowing to implement the hybrid downstream and mid-stream accounting for non-
permanent CCU. New regulated operators, such as e-fuel distributors and waste 
incinerators must obtain ETS permits, prepare MRV plans, report verified emissions, 
and surrender allowances accordingly. 
• The preferred approach for implementation of the IDB would offer a toolbox of 
funding instruments (in terms of allocation method and type of support to be 
provided), allowing policymakers to tailor support to evolving policy priorities and 
industrial needs. With many uncertainties for the industrial decarbonisation projects, 
there is an urgent need to provide support that creates solid business cases, 
administratively simple and with predictable cash flows for the projects. This is why, 
in the early years of the IDB operation (i.e. the ‘Investment Booster’ announced in 
March 2026105, the Commission could start with administrative schemes awarding a 
fixed premium and striking the balance between the EU-level competition and 
national allocations (notably for solidarity purposes). As technologies mature, ETS 
prices increase and regulatory frameworks fully take effect, the Commission could 
move to auctions for a Carbon Contracts for Difference in order to maximise the 
efficiency of the support. This approach could be combined with stronger national 
allocations, in the form of ‘as a service’ contributions or by applying by the 
Commission ‘right of the first use’ to ensure that decarbonisation projects materialise 
in all countries and bring positive spillover effects to them. 
As an exception, PO1 is preferred for carbon removals, as it is considered preferable to 
provide certainty to the ETS sectors on the gross emissions space available and the CDR 
industry on the demand for carbon removals, for the benefit of the EU’s competitiveness.

carbon budgets would be lower. While energy system costs would to an extent be higher, 
system cost impacts could be mitigated through distribution of ETS auctioning revenues and 
supporting policies. Investments, when frontloaded in the decade, would be lower in the last

8.2. 
REFIT (simplification and improved efficiency) 
Since the revision of the ETS Directive in 2018, a number of simplifications have been 
introduced. Article 27a was added to the Directive, allowing Member States to exclude 
installations emitting less than 2,500 tCO2e per year. In addition, a number of 
simplifications, and clarifications have been made through amendments to the Monitoring 
and Reporting Regulation (Commission Implementing Regulation 2018/2066), and 
standardised methodologies and tools have been developed to facilitate implementation. 
These measures are discussed further in the Evaluation Report accompanying this Impact 
Assessment, with a number of possible measures for additional streamlining and 
simplification being proposed (see Annex 15).  
Several of the measures in this Impact Assessment covering emissions from stationary 
installations generally do not involve simplification, since they are considering expanding 
the scope of EU ETS. However, they entail a simplification from a broader perspective, as 
EU-wide climate action should prevent a piece-meal approach of national or subnational 
measures that add complexity to the internal market. In addition, the carbon leakage 
prevention measures assessed consider simplifying the provision of free allocation by not 
updating the carbon leakage list for Phase V (2031-2040), thereby eliminating 
administrative burden for operators. The option of expanding conditionality in free 
allocation would involve some increase in administrative effort, which has to be weighed 
against the environmental benefits of conditionality and the value of the free allocation (see 
Section 6.2.1.6). Annex 12 considers the case for including more small installations within 
EU ETS by lowering the 20 MW thermal capacity threshold. However, it concludes that 
such an expansion would not be proportionate since most of the relevant emissions will be 
covered by EU ETS2.  
The evaluation carried out on the implementation of the MRV Maritime Regulation (see 
Annex 15) confirmed that the MRV system functions as a robust framework, which has

9. HOW WILL ACTUAL IMPACTS BE MONITORED AND EVALUATED? 
The Commission will continue to monitor and evaluate the functioning of the ETS in its 
annual Carbon Market Report, as foreseen under Article 10(5) of the ETS Directive. This 
covers also the impacts of the current revision of the ETS.  
Furthermore, evaluation of progress on the application of the ETS Directive is regulated in 
the current Article 21, which requires Member States to submit to report to the Commission 
on issues including the allocation of allowances, operation of the Registry, application of 
monitoring and reporting, verification and accreditation and issues relating to compliance.  
The measures above (namely the Commission’s annual Carbon Market Report and Member 
States reporting) shall also apply to the sectors to which emissions trading is extended. The 
MRV data obtained through the regulation of the new sectors will be a key source for 
information for the Commission to evaluate progress in the sectors concerned.  
With respect to maritime transport, the Commission will notably rely on data collected 
through the EU maritime transport MRV system and analysis from the annual report on CO2 
emissions from maritime transport, which provides aggregated and explained results. With 
respect to the possible extension to buildings and transport, the Commission will rely on 
data collected through the new MRV system for these sectors, while comparing them also 
with the corresponding GHG inventory data for these sectors.  
The integrated governance and monitoring process under the Regulation on the Governance 
of the Energy Union and Climate action is also expected to make sure that climate and 
energy-related actions at European, as well as regional, national and local level, including 
the ETS, contribute to the EU climate neutrality and Energy Union's objectives.  
Several market analysts regularly closely follow various aspects of the carbon market and 
its functioning, and the Commission will continue to monitor this work. Also, through 
regular contacts with stakeholders, the Commission is alert to their views and concerns about 
the functioning of the ETS. ETS-related matters are discussed in a dedicated forum, the
