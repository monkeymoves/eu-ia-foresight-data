INTRODUCTION: POLITICAL AND LEGAL CONTEXT 
EU merger control – based on Council Regulation (EC) No 139/2004 of 20 January 2004 
on the control of concentrations between undertakings (the “EU Merger Regulation”), its 
implementing Regulation14 and related Notices and Guidelines – aims at ensuring that 
corporate reorganisations in the form of concentrations do not result in lasting damage to 
competition in the internal market.  
Under the EU Merger Regulation, the Commission has exclusive jurisdiction to assess 
whether concentrations with an EU dimension (as defined based on turnover thresholds), 
as well as concentrations referred to it by national competition authorities (“NCAs”), 
would significantly impede effective competition in the internal market or a significant 
part of it.15 Parties to a concentration are required to notify to the Commission all 
concentrations having an EU dimension, by completing a specific notification form. 
Member States are involved in all cases notified to the Commission. They receive copies 
of all notifications, can request a referral of cases to or from the Commission and are 
explicitly consulted in all cases in which an in-depth investigation is launched. 
Pending the Commission’s review and until the Commission’s approval, the parties to 
the concentration cannot implement their concentration. To carry out the review, the EU 
Merger Regulation grants the Commission powers to investigate these concentrations and 
to collect all necessary information, including from the parties to the concentration and 
third parties, for instance from the customers, competitors and suppliers of the parties to 
the concentration. At the end of the merger control review process, the Commission may 
authorise the concentration as initially planned, authorise the concentration subject to 
commitments offered by the merging companies to remedy any competition problems 
identified by the Commission, or prohibit the concentration. The majority of 
concentrations notified to the Commission (93% of all notified cases in 2021) do not 
raise competition concerns and receive unconditional clearance.  
Since 1989 (when the EU merger control regime was introduced), the Commission has 
sought to focus its investigations on the most relevant cases from a competition 
perspective, which have a significant impact on EU businesses and citizens, while 
reducing the administrative burden on businesses subject to merger review where 
possible -- without impairing effective enforcement. To that end, in 2000 the 
Commission introduced a simplified procedure for categories of concentrations that can 
normally be authorised if there are no special circumstances (“simplified cases” as 
opposed to “normal” or “non-simplified cases” which require a detailed review 
procedure). Under the simplified procedure, notifying parties are required to provide less 
information to the Commission; the Commission generally does not request information

Since cases with a European dimension are defined through turnover criteria without any 
assessment of the actual effect of the respective concentrations on competition, many 
concentrations that are notifiable to the Commission under the EU Merger Regulation are 
unlikely to result in lasting damage to competition in the internal market. The simplified 
procedure aims to capture these cases. It is based on certain presumptions, in particular 
based on market shares, but also on size measured in turnover and balance sheet figures 
in the case of joint ventures. These presumptions, which have developed over the years 
based on experience gained, are used to formulate certain categories of cases, which 
normally do not lead to competition issues. There are currently five such categories. 
Three of them are based on market shares, with upper limits of not more than 20% for 
horizontal overlaps and not more than 30% for vertical relationships, as well as below 
50% if the increment results in an HHI below 150. One category is based on turnover and 
balance sheet below EUR 100 million for joint ventures, and the last one is about 
changes from joint to sole control. Since these categories are built on presumptions, the 
Commission reserves the right to revert to the normal procedure via so-called safeguard 
mechanisms. 
Any concentration which is not subject to the simplified procedure is treated under the 
normal procedure. The difference between the two procedures is the level of scrutiny 
applied by the Commission. As normal cases have a higher propensity to lead to 
competition problems in one or more markets, the information requirements in the 
notification form are higher, the Commission will ask more questions in the pre-
notification period and may also, following the formal notification, do a targeted or full 
market investigation asking information from third parties. In practice, in all normal 
cases the Commission contacts third parties to gather information via written 
questionnaires and/or calls.   
Conversely, in concentrations falling under the simplified procedure, the Commission 
employs a lighter approach, which leads to gains on both sides, in particular less 
information to be submitted to the Commission both by parties to the concentration and 
third parties, a faster administrative process and an earlier adoption of the clearance 
decision. The evaluation of procedural and jurisdictional aspects of EU merger control 
launched in 2016 (the “Evaluation”) concluded that parties to  concentration saved on 
average more than 25 working days (that is to say more than 5 calendar weeks) overall 
(including pre-notification and notification) in cases treated under the simplified 
procedure.

documents took stock of issues concerning the substantive assessment of EU merger

2. 
PROBLEM DEFINITION 
2.1. 
What is/are the problems? 
The evaluation of procedural and jurisdictional aspects of EU merger control (the 
“Evaluation”) showed that the 2013 Simplification Package has been effective in better 
focussing the Commission’s resources on the most relevant cases and in reducing 
administrative burden for the businesses involved (i.e. the notifying parties) and 
Commission alike without negatively affecting the effectiveness of EU merger control. 
At the same time, it revealed that there is still some room for improvement, although 
limited. Cases falling under the simplified procedure accounted for more than 77% of the 
notifications each year since 2018 (2018: 77.0%; 2019: 78.8%; 2020: 79.2%; 
2021:78.2%), while in 2013 they accounted for 62.2%. Therefore, as already indicated in 
the Evaluation, the scope to further simplify and capture more cases under the simplified 
procedure is limited. 
Concretely, the Evaluation made clear that there may be additional cases that are 
typically unproblematic which are currently not captured by the simplified procedure and 
that information requirements may be too extensive in certain circumstances. This creates 
unnecessary burden and costs, for the parties to the concentration and for the 
Commission, and leads to unnecessary delays. More specifically, there are currently 
certain categories of cases that are unnecessarily assessed under the normal procedure 
given that they are typically unproblematic and could instead be assessed under the 
simplified procedure, benefitting from a faster and more streamlined review. Although 
this concerns a limited number of cases, it creates unnecessary burden on companies that 
could benefit from a faster and streamlined review. This burden may be very significant 
for parties that cannot benefit from the simplified procedure while the case does not merit 
an in-depth analysis. This initiative aims at addressing this shortcoming.  
It should also be noted that if additional categories of cases  falling under the simplified 
procedure are added, they will obviously have an impact on the number of cases treated 
under the simplified procedure, but also, importantly, on the number of horizontal 
overlaps and vertical relationships that are assessed under the normal procedure. With 
regard to cases falling under the normal procedure, all horizontal overlaps and vertical 
relationships not falling under one of the categories of the Notice on the Simplified 
Procedure are assessed under the normal procedure. This implies that the notifying 
parties need to provide detailed information about each of the markets, the Commission 
needs to assess them and typically, third parties will be asked to respond  to questions 
relating to these markets including in the Commission’s market investigation 
questionnaires. Horizontal overlaps and vertical relationships falling under one of the 

<!-- page:11 -->

categories of the Notice on the Simplified Procedure are not assessed under the normal 
procedure. This means that the notifying parties do not provide information on these 
markets or at least provide significantly less information, the Commission does not 
assess them and third parties do not need to respond to questions about these markets. 
Moreover, the simplified procedure can be further streamlined, in particular by reducing 
unnecessary information requirements. With regard to the normal procedure, it also 
appears possible to reduce certain information requirements, although to a more limited 
extent. At the same time, the Notice on Simplified Procedure may not be clear enough in 
identifying the special circumstances in which cases that meet the requirements of 
simplified treatment may nonetheless require a more detailed review. This means that 
certain cases falling under the simplified procedure, due to specific exceptional 
circumstances, could merit a more detailed review under the normal procedure. However, 
these circumstances are not sufficiently clear in the Notice on Simplified Procedure and 
in particular, in the notification forms. Furthermore, while the Implementing Regulation 
prescribes the format of merger notifications as “[o]ne signed original on paper”, due to 
the exceptional measures that have been taken in the context of the Covid-19 pandemic, 
the Commission has been accepting notifications in digital format, a practice which has 
been widely preferred by both notifying parties and the Commission alike. Society is 
becoming increasingly digital. Contributing to accelerate technological change and hyper 
connectivity by switching to a fully electronic notification system and electronic 
transmission of documents in general might therefore be appropriate. The current system 
obliges notifying parties to print thousands of pages and bring them physically to the 
dependencies of the Commission. This causes an unnecessary negative environmental 
impact and creates administrative costs for businesses. The reduced need for paper as a 
result of the electronic transmission of documents has a  positive impact on the 
environment and on sustainable development goal number 12 (responsible 
consumption)22, although very limited, and is in line with the Commission’s digital 
Strategy. 
In the Public Consultation, several respondents indicated that as regards simplified cases, 
the information requirements are still disproportionate, and that the Short Form CO (the 
document by which simplified cases must be notified to the Commission) is not easy to 
fill in. Regarding normal cases, some respondents voiced concerns about the excessive 
information requirements in the current Form CO (the document by which normal cases 
must be notified to the Commission) and the consequent length of the pre-notification 
process.  At the same time, on several occasions, the Commission receives calls to look 
more in depth into certain transactions with limited or no overlaps between the parties’ 
activities, but which do raise important nascent/potential competition, innovation or other 
issues.23

2.2. 
What are the problem drivers? 
The Evaluation showed that the current legal framework implies that the simplified 
procedure does not capture all cases that are typically unproblematic and vice versa that 
it may not be clear enough when cases that meet the requirements for simplified 
treatment require nonetheless a more detailed review. Furthermore, information 
requirements might at times be too far-reaching for unproblematic cases. In addition, the 
current legal framework would need to be amended to allow for a fully electronic 
notification system and electronic transmission of documents in general. 
The problems identified above are mainly due to shortcomings in the current legal 
framework. In particular, the Notice on Simplified Procedure does not cover certain 
categories of cases, which are currently unnecessarily assessed under the normal 
procedure given that they are typically unproblematic and could be assessed under the 
simplified procedure, benefitting from a faster and streamlined review. Moreover, the 
mandatory notification forms contained in the annexes to the Implementing Regulation 
require information that may not be necessary in particular for cases falling under the 
simplified procedure and, to a much lesser extent, for cases assessed under the normal 
procedure. Finally, the current legal framework does not allow fully electronic 
notifications, thus creating an unnecessary negative environmental impact and additional 
administrative costs for notifying parties. 
2.3. 
How likely is the problem to persist? 
If the current legal framework is kept in place, the current situation will persist and 
unnecessary costs and burdens will continue to be borne by notifying parties.  
3. 
WHY SHOULD THE EU ACT? 
3.1. 
Legal basis 
EU competition law, including EU merger control, is an exclusive competence of the 
European Union. The Commission adopted the Implementing Regulation on the basis of 
the EU Merger Regulation. The Commission published its first notice introducing a 
simplified merger procedure in 2000. The latest review of the Implementing Regulation 
and the Notice on Simplified Procedure was carried out as part of the 2013 Simplification 
Package. 
3.2. 
Subsidiarity: Necessity and added value of EU action 
As mentioned above, EU action is necessary as EU competition law, including EU 
merger control, is an exclusive competence of the European Union. 
4. 
OBJECTIVES: WHAT IS TO BE ACHIEVED? 
4.1. 
General objectives 
This initiative aims at revising the Implementing Regulation, with annexes for a revised 
Form CO, Short Form CO, Form RS and Form RM, as well as the Notice on Simplified 
Procedure. The general objective of the initiative is to improve EU merger control 

<!-- page:13 -->
 
12 
 
procedures which aim at preventing lasting damage to competition in the internal market 
stemming from anti-competitive mergers, by providing clearer rules and guidance that 
are fit-for-purpose and simplify/streamline procedures where appropriate to reduce the 
administrative burden for parties involved and the Commission alike, without increasing 
the risk of negative effects on the enforcement of EU merger control rules. This will 
allow parties to get legal certainty more quickly and at a lower cost and will as such be 
beneficial for their fundamental rights of defence, and will allow the Commission to 
allocate resources more appropriately.  
4.2. 
Specific objectives 
The specific objectives of this initiative are to (i) better target the merger review process, 
allowing the Commission to focus its investigations on the cases that merit a more 
detailed review and (ii) reduce the administrative costs and burdens of the merger review 
process. 
5. 
WHAT ARE THE AVAILABLE POLICY OPTIONS? 
5.1. 
What is the baseline from which options are assessed? 
The baseline from which the options are assessed is the current legal framework 
regarding merger control by the European Commission. Currently, the relevant legal 
framework includes (in addition to the EU Merger Regulation) the Implementing 
Regulation and the Notice on Simplified Procedure (as last updated in 2013).

5.2. 
Description of the policy options 
5.2.1. Introduction and scoping of the options 
The Evaluation indicated that there are considerable complexities in achieving significant 
simplification through a review of the EU Merger Regulation (such as exempting certain 
transactions from merger review or introducing a system of self-assessment) without 
compromising the effectiveness or the efficiency of the EU merger control regime.  
In light of the results of the Evaluation and the Commission’s commitment to focus its 
detailed investigations on the most relevant cases and reduce administrative burden for 
those cases which are unlikely to raise competition concerns, the Commission wishes to 
explore options for further targeting and simplifying the review, in both simplified and, 
where possible, non-simplified cases without compromising effective merger 
enforcement.  
In this context, several alternative scenarios have been identified and considered, but 
have been rejected upfront. 
Scenario 1: Reform of the EU Merger Regulation. This option would include a reform of 
the legal framework regarding the substantive assessment of mergers (namely those 
aspects related to the examination of whether or not a notified concentration significantly 
impedes effective competition in the internal market − and, if so, how to remedy it). 
However, the results of the Evaluation did not support this option. In line with the 
findings of the 2014 White Paper, the evaluation pointed out that the EU Merger 
Regulation overall provides a good framework for effectively preventing anticompetitive 
effects of mergers and acquisitions in the internal market, so that improvements are only 
appropriate on certain specific issues. As such, this option was rejected. 
Scenario 2: Setting up a self-assessment system with the possibility of a voluntary 
notification for selected categories of cases. The Evaluation again did not support this 
option. It showed that this option would come with issues, related to reducing legal 
certainty; the problem of undoing closed transactions; the mechanics of the referral 
system in a voluntary system at EU level; and the fact that a self-assessment system can 
work for clear-cut criteria such as turnover thresholds but would be more problematic for 
more subjective criteria, such as potential harm to competition. As such, this option was 
not upheld. 

<!-- page:15 -->
 
14 
 
Scenario 3: Excluding extra-EEA joint ventures from the scope of application of the EU 
Merger Regulation (a proposal contained in the 2014 White Paper). The Evaluation 
showed complaints about the requirement to notify joint ventures that have no impact in 
the EEA, while the option of further streamlining the review of extra-EEA joint ventures 
was welcomed. However, the Evaluation revealed that excluding extra-EEA joint 
ventures entirely from the scope of EU merger control entailed the risk that these 
transactions may fall under different national regimes and therefore trigger multiple 
notifications in different Member States, which would mean losing the benefit of the one-
stop-shop review at EU level of these transactions, increasing burden and costs on 
notifying parties rather than reducing them. Therefore, the possibility to exclude extra-
EEA joint ventures from the obligation to notify was not considered an option. At the 
same time, given their lack of impact on the internal market, information requirements 
for these cases should be limited to the maximum extent possible. 
In addition to the rejected options listed above, the Evaluation also resulted in a number 
of valid policy options for further simplification, related to (i) expanding and clarifying 
the categories of cases treated under the simplified procedure, (ii) streamlining the review 
of cases treated under the simplified procedure, (iii) streamlining the review of cases 
treated under the normal procedure, and (iv) electronic transmissions of documents.  
In more detail:  
i. 
As regards expanding and clarifying the categories of simplified cases, the 
Commission’s initiative looks to identify new categories of simplified cases, and to 
introduce a flexibility mechanism that would allow the Commission to treat under 
the simplified procedure cases which a priori do not fall under any of the simplified 
treatment categories. 
ii. 
On the streamlining of the review of simplified cases, the Commission is proposing 
certain categories of simplified cases which would not require any pre-notification 
contacts. The initiative also looks to reduce the information required in simplified 
cases (in particular regarding jurisdiction). To that end, the Commission proposes 
to move for simplified cases to a notification form with a tick-the-box format and 
less open text. 
iii. 
Regarding the streamlining of the review of non-simplified cases, based on the 
experience gained by the Commission in its enforcement practice over the years, 
here the Commission proposes to reduce the information required for some types of 
non-simplified cases. The Commission also looks to provide clear instructions to 
notifying parties who plan to request waivers for certain sections of the notification 
form.  
iv. 
The Commission is currently allowing businesses to notify merger cases 
electronically echoing Covid-19 restrictions. Per this initiative, the Commission 
now looks to establish that going forward, notifications of mergers and 
transmission of documents in general shall take place in principle through digital 
means – providing for a safe, reliable, environmentally-friendly, and cost-efficient 
transmission mechanism. The reduced need for paper as a result of the electronic

transmission of documents also entails a positive impact on sustainable 
development goals number 12 (responsible consumption)25, although very limited. 
The problems identified are mainly due to shortcomings in the current legal framework. 
In particular, the Notice on Simplified Procedure does not cover certain categories of 
cases, which are currently unnecessarily assessed under the normal procedure given that 
they are typically unproblematic and could be assessed under the simplified procedure, 
benefitting from a faster and streamlined review. Moreover, the mandatory notification 
forms contained in the annexes to the Implementing Regulation require information that 
may not be necessary in particular for cases falling under the simplified procedure and, to 
a much lesser extent, for cases assessed under the normal procedure. Finally, the current

Therefore, soft measures such as guidance to case teams or to notifying parties could not 
achieve the objectives of this initiative in an effective manner.

5.2.4. Policy options regarding streamlining the review of cases treated

Two policy options have been identified for this area of streamlining the review process.  
Option 1: Introducing modifications to the structure of the current notification form,

majority of replies received considered this separation as having no or negligible effects

Waivers are already part of the current notification process but are not currently being 
requested in many cases. The current Form CO provides in its introduction general 
guidance on when, how and for what information typically a waiver can be requested by 
the notifying party or parties (1.4.g), and points in a very scattered way to information 
requested in the Form CO which is particularly suitable for a waiver request. However, 
the Form CO does not highlight these instances in any obvious or specifically visible 
manner. Instead, the encouragement to request a waiver in relation to certain information 
requirements is included only in nine footnotes in the current Form CO. This may have 
contributed to the fact that notifying parties have seldom made use of this opportunity to 
save time and effort in filling in the Form CO. 
In the revised Form CO, a new section on waivers would provide more prominence to the 
possibility of requesting waivers and a stronger encouragement for notifying parties to 
make use of this possibility. The new section on waivers would set out two general 
conditions for waiver requests to be considered favourably by the Commission: (i) the 
notifying parties give adequate reasons why the relevant information is not reasonably 
available and provide best estimates for the missing data; (ii) the notifying parties give 
adequate reasons why the relevant information is not necessary for the examination of the 
case. If one of these two conditions were fulfilled, the notifying party would have a good 
chance to obtain the waiver.  
The new specific section on waivers would also clearly outline which parts of the Form 
CO are particularly suitable for a waiver request. For instance, a waiver for the entire 
chapter 10 is proposed, which is about product differentiation and closeness of 
competition.  
As a safeguard against under-enforcement, however, the Commission would retain the 
right to ask for the information during the administrative procedure should this become 
necessary to carry out a complete assessment of the case. As before, waivers would be 
granted following a case-by-case assessment, but the clarified process would make it 
more likely that notifying parties request waivers and that the Commission ultimately 
grants them.

the Commission temporarily accepts and in fact, encourages, merger notifications in 
digital format. However, following the submission of the electronic notification, in order 
to comply with the existing Implementing Regulation, notifying parties are still obliged 
to deliver a duly signed paper copy of the notification to the Commission, without delay.

Two options have been identified, taking advantage of DG Competition’s experience 
regarding electronic notifications, to further promote the Commission’s digital 
transformation and to simplify the notification process for the notifying parties and the 
general exchange of case-related documents between the Commission and external 
stakeholders. 
Option 1: A system of electronic notifications followed by paper originals without delay 
(i.e. as soon as possible).  
Option 2: Accept fully digital transmission of documents, including transmission of

The two options are alternatives.

initially be introduced using the Commission’s web-based exchange platform, eTrustEx.

there must be a strong link between the two. For instance, a USB drive containing 1 
million documents cannot be considered as validly signed if it is hand-delivered to the 
Commission together with a hand-signed cover page. In this case, the link between the

notification, including annexes (or any other documents), is provided to the Commission

copy is required. 
In addition, under Option 2, it may be appropriate for the Commission to accept, as a 
back-up solution, a hand signed paper copy of the complete submission, to be hand 
delivered or sent to the Commission by registered post. Option 2 remains the “digital by 
default” option and a paper notification may be accepted only exceptionally, for instance, 
where the notifying party cannot possibly sign electronically, or the validity of the digital 
signature cannot be verified by the Commission.

The following sub-sections assess the impact of the different policy options on: (i)the 
internal market. EU competition policy has contributed significantly over the years to the 
competitiveness of the EU economy and European businesses. It helps create the

involved will make them more efficient preparing for and going through the merger 
review process, thereby making them more competitive. However, given the small 
number of businesses involved (in the hundreds) in a given year, the overall impact is 
likely to be extremely limited and therefore will not be further discussed.

HOW DO THE OPTIONS COMPARE? 
This section compares the different policy options and sub-options between themselves 
where relevant and against the baseline scenario as regards their effectiveness, efficiency 
and coherence, and concludes on the preferred option for each area of 
simplification/streamlining. This comparison takes into account the impact of each option 
on the internal market; on businesses and enforcement agencies; and on digitalisation as 
explained in section 6 above. 
In order to compare the effectiveness of the different policy options, their ability to 
achieve the specific objectives of this initiative are assessed, i.e., to:  
(a) 
better target the merger review process, allowing the Commission to focus its 
investigations on the cases that merit a more detailed review and 
(b) 
reduce the administrative costs and burdens of the merger review process (for 
businesses and for the Commission). 
 
The assessment of efficiency takes into account how the policy options will affect the 
costs that businesses incur in order to notify a transaction. For this initiative, considering 
that one of its objectives is to reduce administrative costs for businesses, there is an 
overlap between the assessment of effectiveness and efficiency. Therefore, the 
assessment of effectiveness and efficiency will be presented together in the remainder of 
this section. 
As regards coherence, the policy options need to be assessed in light of other EU 
legislation with relevance for the merger review process.

ANNEX 3 - WHO IS AFFECTED AND HOW? 
1. Practical Implications of the Initiative  
 
The initiative consists in revising the existing EU merger procedural rules, namely the 
Implementing Regulation (with its Annexes and accompanying documents) and the 
Notice on Simplified Procedure (together, the “legal texts”). As such, the initiative is 
largely a continuation of the existing rules, which the Evaluation fundamentally 
confirmed to be useful and relevant for stakeholders.  
 
In the assessment of the practical implications of the initiative, it is important to recall 
that the legal texts do not impose any additional compliance obligations on businesses 
beyond those reflected in the EU Merger Regulation.  On the contrary, the rules aim to 
facilitate the work of businesses that have to apply the EU Merger Regulation and notify 
their concentrations prior to implementation.  
 
The revised legal texts will primarily have practical implications for businesses and, 
indirectly, the law firms and other professionals advising businesses on compliance with 
the EU Merger Regulation. The revised legal texts will also have implications for the 
NCAs and national courts. Indirectly, the revised legal texts will also affect consumers, 
but the initiative has no practical implications for them. 
 
1.1. Businesses  
 
The stakeholder group that would primarily be affected by the Commission’s merger 
simplification/streamlining initiative is businesses, as they are the stakeholders that 
mostly use the legal texts regarding their concentrations.  In practice, businesses use the 
Notice on Simplified Procedure to self-assess their concentrations and determine whether 
they should fall under the simplified or the non-simplified procedure.  Businesses also 
use the Implementing Regulation and its Annexes to notify concentrations to the 
Commission, to refer cases to the Commission under Article 4(5) of the EU Merger 
Regulation, and/or to explain/describe the remedies proposed for concentrations that 
could give rise to competition concerns.  
 
In the Evaluation of the current rules, the Commission found that stakeholders generally 
agreed that there was some, possibly limited, room to further simplify merger procedures 
and reduce red tape.  The initiative to revise the legal texts addressed problems of scope, 
clarity, and complexity of the relevant rules.  Additional clarity and rules that are simpler 
would greatly increase the level of legal certainty provided, which would in turn increase 
the ability of the rules to facilitate efficient notification and investigation of 
concentrations before the Commission.  Finally, the reduced need for paper as a result of 
the electronic transmission of documents also entails a positive impact on sustainable 
development goal number 12 (responsible consumption), although very limited. 
 
Indirectly, law firms and other professionals advising businesses on compliance with the 
EU Merger Regulation would also benefit from a clearer set of rules, which would allow 
them to more effectively advise businesses. 
 

<!-- page:81 -->

The benefits of the different policy options for businesses were explained in more detail 
in Section 6 of this report.  
 
1.2. Commission  
 
This initiative would also have practical implications for the Commission, which applies 
the EU merger procedural rules.  The Commission would have to take into account these 
rules to:  
 
 determine whether a case should be treated under the simplified or the non-simplified

 confirm that a notification (or other submission) is complete and correctly signed and

 organise the merger review procedure.  
 
The impact of the initiative on enforcement authorities is explained in more detail in 
Section 6 of the report.  
 
1.3. Consumers 
 
The initiative is not expected to have direct practical implications for consumers, as the 
legal texts do not apply to them and are usually not used by them in any relevant manner. 
Indirectly, however, consumers would be affected by the initiative, in that they will 
continue to benefit from effective competition (in terms of prices, quality, choice, and 
innovation) as the Commission will be able to dedicate its resources and intervene in 
mergers raising competition concerns (unlike simplified cases).

on the costs and benefits of the identified policy options.  It should be noted from the 
outset that while savings for businesses involved in a notification (notifying parties) are 
significant, the aggregate impact of this initiative on businesses is limited, given the 
relatively small number of companies compared to the number of overall existing 
companies that notify a transaction to the Commission every year: in the last 5 years, 
there have been less than 400 notifications per year, which include most often only one 
notifying party. Moreover, the additional number of cases captured by the simplified 
procedure will be limited.

businesses, asking stakeholders on several occasions to quantify these benefits and cost 
reductions. However, it proved impossible to collect sufficient quantitative evidence on 
the costs and benefits of the identified policy options. The fact that it was not possible to

ANNEX 4 - ISSUES OF A TECHNICAL NATURE RELATING TO THE CLARITY, 
COMPLETENESS AND COMPLEXITY OF THE RULES 
As explained in Section 5.1 of the report, the current legal framework includes (in 
addition to the EU Merger Regulation) the Implementing Regulation and the Notice on 
Simplified Procedure (as last updated in 2013).  In addition to expanding the simplified 
categories; streamlining the review of simplified and non-simplified cases; and 
introducing electronic transmissions of documents, the Evaluation showed that further 
targeting of EU merger control should be considered, by clarifying and revising merger 
procedural rules (e.g., in the Implementing Regulation).  
Against this background, together with the revisions in the legal texts reflecting the 
preferred options as described in Section 7 of the report, the Commission amended 
provisions of the Implementing Regulation to clarify and complete them.  Key among 
these amendments are the following:  
a. Article 2 of the draft revised Implementing Regulation.  Under the existing 
Implementing Regulation, Article 2 includes paragraph 3 which reads: “[j]oint 
notifications shall be submitted by a joint representative who is authorised to 
transmit and receive documents on behalf of all notifying parties”.  However, in the 
Commission’s decisional practice, joint notifications are also accepted when each of 
the notifying parties have a separate representative who signs the notification.  Thus, 
Article 2(3) was removed.  
  
b. Article 9 of the draft Implementing Regulation. This article provides for the 
suspension of time limits under certain circumstances and details the circumstances 
under which the suspension ends, including inter alia where the parties fail to provide 
information requested by the Commission. The proposed amendment aims to clarify 
that the Commission has the possibility to restart the clock ex officio where the 
information requested is no longer necessary. 
 
c. Article 13(2) of the draft revised Implementing Regulation.  The existing 
Implementing Regulation makes reference to the statement of objections, a document 
that the Commission can address to the notifying parties in the course of merger 
investigations.  In response to this document, the notifying parties can provide 
comments exercising their rights of defence.68  In its decisional practice, after sending 
a statement of objections, the Commission sometimes sends a supplementary 
statement of objections.  This raises new objections or modifies the intrinsic nature of 
the objections previously raised.  Article 13(2) was amended to include a reference to 
supplementary statements of objections.   
 
d. Article 13(4) of the draft revised Implementing Regulation.  In its decisional practice, 
after sending a statement of objections, the Commission sometimes sends a letter of

ANNEX 5: REVIEW OF INTERVENTION CASES IN THE PERIOD 2015-2020 
1. One of the internal research work streams that we have conducted concerns the 
review of past cases in which the Commission intervened in the period from 2015 
until 2020 (a total of 127 cases). The purpose of this review is to assess the risks of 
introducing new categories of simplified cases (i.e. to identify whether any of the past 
intervention cases in the relevant period would have been caught by any of the 
proposed categories). We summarize the findings of our research in the following 
sections. 
 
1. Interventions in  horizontal mergers 
 
1.1. Flexibility clause  
 
2. We propose to introduce in the Notice on Simplified Procedure a flexibility clause for 
horizontal overlaps with combined market shares between 20 and 25% and for 
vertical relationships with combined markets shares between 30 and 35%. 
3. The internal research showed that in 23 cases out of the total 127 interventions (i.e. in 
18% of the interventions), the Commission intervened in some markets where the 
parties had horizontal overlaps with combined market shares below 25%. More 
specifically, we found the following situations: 
a) In nine cases, there were some intervention markets with market shares between 
20 and 25% in some market segments (some of them even below the current 20% 
threshold) but market shares where significantly higher in other segments. Given 
that the 25% threshold was exceeded in certain segments, the flexibility clause 
would not have been applicable. 
b) In eight cases, there were some national markets with market shares between 20 
and 25% in some Member States where we intervened, but many other national 
markets with significantly higher shares (this is for instance the case in several 
pharmaceutical mergers). In these cases, the remedy typically covered all the 
EEA for viability reasons. Therefore, the impact of any flexibility clause in these 
cases would have been very limited. 
c) In six cases, there were some intervention markets with market shares between 20 
and 25% (some of them even below 20%), but in these markets there were less 
than three significant competitors left post-transaction. To avoid that these 
markets escape the Commission’s review further to the introduction of a 
flexibility clause, a new safeguard should be introduced, requiring for the 
application of the simplified procedure that at least three significant competitors 
remain in the market post-merger. Moreover, we will require notifying parties to 
systematically provide market shares for at least three competitors for the three 
years prior to the transaction so that these situation can be spot early in the 
process and submit these markets to a full review under the normal procedure, 
rather than treating them under the simplified procedure.   

<!-- page:92 -->
 
91 
 
d) In three cases, there were some intervention markets with market shares between 
20 and 25% (or even below 20%) in the year prior to the transaction, but the 
combined  market shares were higher in previous years. We currently already 
require market shares for the last three financial years prior to each transaction, so 
these situations are also easy to identify. 
e) In three cases, the market shares provided by the parties were not reliable and the 
Commission had to carry out a market reconstruction. In certain cases, the parties 
presented very low market shares, which were unreliable. However, the 
Commission was able to identify the problem and relied on a market 
reconstruction or other estimates.  The current safeguards and exceptions in the 
Notice on Simplified Procedure already indicate that the simplified procedure 
should not apply where market shares are unreliable. This should continue to be 
the case in a revised Notice on Simplified Procedure. Therefore, the introduction 
of the envisaged flexibility clause should not have a significant impact in these 
situations.  
f) In three cases, there were some intervention markets with market shares below 
25%, but one of the Parties was a recent entrant. To avoid that these markets 
escape the Commission’s attention further to the introduction of a flexibility 
clause, a specific safeguard should be introduced in the Notice on Simplified 
Procedure, which excludes in principle the application of the simplified procedure 
for cases where one of the parties is a recent entrant. This would again allow the 
Commission to easily spot these situations early in the process.  
g) In two cases  in which there were hundreds of overlap markets, the parties had 
combined market shares between 20 and 25% (or even below 20%) on a limited 
number of overlap markets. Remedies in these cases, however, addressed all 
concerns even in these markets, so the impact of any flexibility clause would 
again have been very limited. 
h) In one case, there were some intervention markets with market shares below 25%, 
but with pipeline overlaps. Again, to avoid any risk of overlooking such cases and 
treating them as simplified under the flexibility clause, we should introduce a 
specific safeguard for this situation for pipeline to pipeline or pipeline to 
marketed products overlaps, allowing the Commission teams to easily spot these 
situations early in the process. 
2. Interventions in vertical cases 
 
2.1. Flexibility clause 
 
4. The internal research showed that we intervened in three cases (2% of the total 
number of interventions in the period 2015-2020) on some markets with vertical 
relationships with combined markets shares between 30-35%. However, none of 
these cases would have completely fallen under the simplified procedure with the 
flexibility clause, as there were other markets with higher market shares. Moreover, 
the flexibility clause would not have had any impact on any of the markets in which

we intervened. In each of the three cases concerned, we found markets which 
presented higher market shares depending on segments or geographies (national or 
local markets). In one case, market shares were not reliable given that the parties had 
included a company that was clearly not a competitor (in any event the market shares 
in this case ranged  between 33-39%). Finally, in one case the market share 
thresholds were exceeded in terms of capacity in the upstream market with high 
market shares in some national downstream markets. For the last situation, we 
propose including a specific safeguard in the  Notice on Simplified Procedure which 
excludes the application of the simplified procedure for markets where market share 
thresholds are exceeded in terms of capacity or production. That would again allow 
the Commission to easily spot these situations early in the process. 
 
3. Reference to joint ventures with up to EUR 150 million turnover or assets 
value 
 
5. Our internal research on intervention cases shows that one of the 127 cases in which 
we have intervened in the period 2015-2020 could have fallen under this new 
category. In fact, another case fell under the current threshold, as the turnover of the 
JV was lower than EUR 100 million. However, this was not an impediment to assess 
the case under the normal procedure and ultimately even prohibit the transaction. 
  
6. Including a safeguard requiring the parties to systematically provide market shares 
for all overlap markets, even if the case falls under this simplified category (as it 
currently done in our case practice), it would be easy for the Commission to identify 
any clearly problematic markets that might require reverting the case to the normal 
procedure despite the small size of the joint venture. 
 
4. Conclusion  
 
7. Based on the result of our internal research, none of the 127 interventions in the 
period 2015-2020 would have completely fallen under the simplified procedure with 
the new categories proposed in conjunction with the additional safeguards proposed.
