INTRODUCTION: POLITICAL AND LEGAL CONTEXT 
1.1. 
EU energy and climate objectives 
The EU has longstanding objectives to increase energy efficiency and to reduce its greenhouse gas 
emissions. These go along with other objectives to reduce its environmental impacts. In December 
2019, the Commission presented the European Green Deal1 to strengthen these objectives and as 
the cornerstone of its strategy to fulfil the United Nation’s 2030 Agenda for Sustainable 
Development2. In September 2020, it presented a Climate Target Plan (CTP) for 20303, showing 
the need for a higher contribution of energy efficiency and renewable energy to achieve a net 55% 
GHG emission reduction most cost-effectively, in line with the Paris Agreement. The European 
Parliament and Council subsequently agreed to achieve this level of reduction in GHG emissions. 
The Commission followed this by adopting the EU “Fit for 55” package4 aiming to achieve the 
neceessary cut in GHG emissions. 
One pillar of the CTP and subsequently the ‘Fit for 55’ package is energy efficiency. A revision of 
the Energy Efficiency Directive (EED)5 to increase the overall ambition was proposed. This would 
require Member States to enhance their National Energy and Climate Plans (NECPs) to achieve at 
least an overall 9% reduction in EU energy consumption in 2030 compared to the reference6. The 
EED itself contains limited energy saving measures and in addition to Member State actions, 
additional measures at EU level are needed7. In this context, the ecodesign and energy labelling 
rules for products arise as important instruments to realise EU’s energy and decarbonisation 
objectives8. 
Another pillar of the European Green Deal is a more circular economy. The new Circular Economy 
Action Plan9 sets out steps to work towards this. It aims to reduce product environmental impacts 
for example through promoting longer product lives, greater resource efficiency and enhancing 
recycling and recycled content. Certain of these aspects can be addressed through measures under 
the Ecodesign Directive since decisions made at design can influence the product’s life span and 
ease of maintenance, repair, reuse, upgrade, recyclability and waste handling. The Ecodesign 
Working Plan 2016-2019 identified ecodesign measures’ potential to contribute to circular 
economy objectives and subsequent supporting studies have systematically considered resource 
efficiency aspects.  
Reducing energy use and promoting the circular economy are also important for many other 
reasons10. Lower energy use reduces the EU’s energy import dependence and improves energy 
security, aspects that are particularly relevant in the current context of continuous increase of 
energy prices. On 8 March 2022, the Commission published its Communication “RepowerEU:

1.2. 
The contribution of Ecodesign and Energy labelling 
There are market barriers that hamper the introduction and uptake of more energy efficient and 
more long lasting products. It is desirable for policies to address these barriers since they lead, from 
the point of view of the consumer and society, to unrealised economically viable energy savings. 
Behavioural failures refer to the cognitive limitations and biases that prevent consumers and 
investors to appreciate rationally the benefits of energy efficiency14. Market failures arise from the 
fact that many impacts and aspects of energy supply or resource use are not brought into market 
prices15. Market barriers such as lack of information and awareness or financing challenges result in 
economically rational energy savings not being realised or more sustainable choices not being 
made.  
The EU Ecodesign Directive aims to address these barriers by setting performance requirements to 
remove the worst performing products from the market. The assessment aims to ensure that the 
minimum requirements are set at the level of Least Lifecycle Consumer Cost. These harmonised 
requirements have specifically addressed energy efficiency and have led to significant reductions in 
energy use of household and industrial products. Being set at EU level, they have avoided 
subjecting industry to multiple national rules. 
Energy labelling rules complement ecodesign requirements. These provide information to 
consumers with the aim of encouraging them to purchase products that have a better energy 
performance than the minimum. This has helped consumers to reduce their energy bills by easily 
identifying and comparing more energy efficient appliances. Nearly 80 % of the EU public 
recognise the label and say it has influenced their purchase decision16.

1.3. 
Legal context of the impact assessment 
Following an assessment and preparatory study, local space heaters were regulated under the 
Ecodesign Framework Directive through Commission Regulation (EU) 2015/1188 on ecodesign 
requirements for local space heaters18 (hereafter the LSH Regulation). At the time of adoption, the 
ecodesign and energy labelling requirements for local space heaters were estimated to achieve 
energy savings of approximately 3,8 Mtoe and related GHG emission reductions of 6,7 MtCO2eq 
by 2020. 
Article 7 of the LSH Regulation, mandates a review of the Regulation, in particular regarding the 
possibility to set stricter requirements for energy efficiency and NOx emissions, the modification of 
the verification tolerances of Annex IV, the validity of the correction factors and the 
appropriateness of introducing third-party certification. Consequently a review of the LSH 
Regulation was included in the Ecodesign Working Plan 2016-201919. This noted that the review 
would also assess aspects relevant to the circular economy. The review has been carried out through 
a review study20 and this impact assessment21.

PROBLEM DEFINITION 
2.1. 
What are the problems? 
2.1.1. The first key problem: missed in-use energy savings from local space heaters  
The EU Reference Scenario 2020 projects energy consumption to be reduced to 1.124 Mtoe of 
primary energy and 864 Mtoe of final energy in 2030, which is insufficient to attain the energy 
efficiency target under the fit-for-55 package. As indicated above, the draft revision of the EED sets 
out an additional binding reduction of 9% of energy consumption in the EU from energy efficiency 
measures, which would bring the energy consumption down to 1.023 Mtoe of primary energy and 
787 Mtoe of final energy by 2030.  
The revised EED will mandate Member States to lay down measures at national level to 
collectively contribute to the EU binding target, thus underscoring the importance of co-
responsibility among Member States to reach the global EU energy and climate goals. This 
approach entails the risk that some Member States may not fully realise their national objectives 
and therefore jeopardise the achievement of the 9% target. Ecodesign and energy labelling 
measures (which unlike measures in the scope of the EED have the advantage of being directly 
applicable across the EU) must contribute to that 9% by reducing energy consumption on top of the 
reductions achieved under EED.  
The modelled in-use energy consumption from local space heaters was 15 Mtoe in 202022, 1,7% of 
EU total final energy consumption and 4% of the final energy consumption of households and 
services. In view of this it is desirable to maximise further cost-effective increases in the energy 
efficiency of local space heaters.  
Section 1.5 of the review study indicates that there could be potential for further energy efficiency 
gains through addressing a number of aspects of the current LSH Regulation. In this respect, the 
review study concludes that the LSH Regulation presents barriers that impede gaining further 
energy savings because the legislative scope does not clearly delimit the types of local space 
heaters that must fulfil the ecodesign requirements.  
The contribution of local space heaters to EU energy efficiency objectives can be enhanced by 
clarifying and moderately broadening the scope of the legislation, without technological 
improvement. In fact, section 4.5.3 of the review study identifies that technology is mature and 
unlikely to yield significant energy efficiency improvements, in particular for electric heaters. 
Options for achieving better efficiency all relate to fitting the heaters with suitable controllers. 
Further, section 6 of the review study assesses the possibilities for energy efficiency improvements, 
which may come from either automatic programming or control accuracy, both linked to the use of 
controllers but not to the improvement of the heating technology in itself. There is therefore little to

Besides preventing the realisation of energy savings, the lack of clarity of the scope results in 
unnecessary burden for manufacturers and Market Surveillance Authorities (MSAs). For 
manufacturers, the unclear scope generates competitive disadvantages between manufacturers 
whose products are compliant with the ecodesign rules and those whose products are not, resulting 
in extra costs for compliant manufacturers and hindering access to the market in equal conditions. 
These excess costs are quantified in section 6.5. On the side of MSAs, there is loss of time and 
resources on the selection of the products to be controlled because of the uncertainty about the 
inclusion or not in the scope of the LSH Regulation of specific product categories like underfloor 
heaters. Both industry and Member States declare that the current scope of the LSH Regulation is a 
source of confusion among manufacturers on the one hand, which are not sure about whether their 
products must or not fulfil the ecodesign rules, and national authorities on the other hand, which are 
frequently engaged in discussions with manufacturers about whether their products are in the scope 
of the LSH Regulation and would consequently be subject to market surveillance. 
2.1.2. The second key problem: market failures due to lack of regulatory provisions 
on circular economy 
The lack of regulatory provisions on circular economy lead to negative environmental effects that 
are not internalised in the price of local space heaters. This means that industry does not receive 
sufficiently strong signals to incorporate resource efficiency criteria in the design and manufacture 
of their products. In addition, circular economy and climate and energy policies like energy 
efficiency, have not always been well integrated. For instance, the IPCC’s Summaries for Policy 
Makers hardly mentioned circularity or resource efficiency as a decarbonisation option, and the first 
Circular Economy Action Plan of 2015 vaguely mentioned climate change while acknowledging 
that “to date ecodesign requirements have mainly targeted energy efficiency”. Nowadays, scientific 
literature has demonstrated the connection between circular economy, energy savings and climate 
change, thanks to studies like Material Economics’ and Sitra’s, entitled The circular economy – A 
powerful force for climate mitigation (2018)23, from the UN’s International Resources Panel2425  
and the Commission’s analysis for the Long-Term Strategic Vision to reduce GHG emissions. Now 
these interlinkages are acknowledged at both scientific and policy levels, notably under the 
European Green Deal and its initiatives (e.g. Renovation Wave, EPBD revision).

connected to general heating systems (e.g. for an entire building) by means of central controllers 
that incorporate the necessary functions to make the full system compliant with the LSH 
Regulation. Nevertheless, the exclusion of slave heaters from the scope of the LSH Regulation is a 
source of confusion and conflict between manufacturers and MSAs.  
First, the review study points out in section 1.5.2 that stakeholders have different opinions on 
whether underfloor heaters are slave heaters and should therefore be excluded from the scope of 
the LSH Regulation. Contrary to the definition of slave heaters, many underfloor heaters such as 
heating cables, do not get any signal from the controller because they are simple resistors that do 
not have the capacity to receive and interpret any electronic signal, but are just connected to a 
thermostat that autonomously switches on and off the current. These underfloor heaters are 
therefore not slave heaters, but they are placed on the market as such and are therefore exempted 
from the ecodesign measures.  
Second, there are also doubts about whether electric towel heaters, also called towel rails, are in 
the scope of the LSH Regulation. This is because the LSH Regulation considers local space heaters 
as devices whose purpose is to ensure the thermal comfort of human beings. As explained in 
section 1.5 of the review study, towel rails are not only intended to heat towels but also to heat the 
space in which they are placed. In reality, most towel heaters are designed with enough power to 
easily heat most bathrooms without any additional heat source. There are national differences, but 
particularly in Member States with high shares of electric heating like France, towel heaters fulfil 
this double purpose of heating towels and room at the same time. However, only around 10%26 of 
towel rails are compliant because the majority of products are declared as intended exclusively to 
heat towels and therefore do not fulfil the ecodesign rules. Apart from the missed energy savings, 
section 1.5.2 of the review study indicates that this situation creates a distortion between those 
manufacturers that consider the product in the scope and those for whom towel rails are outside of 
the scope. 
Finally, many local space heaters are placed on the market separately from the controller just to 
escape the application of the LSH Regulation.  
2.2.2. Restrictive scope of the LSH Regulation 
The LSH Regulation excludes tube heaters27 above 120 kW heat output power. As explained in 
section 1.5.3 of the review study there is a historic reason for this exclusion, namely that the LSH 
Regulation follows the same approach as the EN 416 standard, which also excluded tube heaters 
above 120 kW before it was amended. Nevertheless, the latest version of the standard now includes 
tube heaters between 120 and 300 kW, whose potential energy savings are significant enough to 
include those devices in the scope of the LSH Regulation, ensuring also coherence with the tests 
and results set in the standards for these products.  
The LSH Regulation is also restrictive with respect to low power modes. The formula for the 
calculation of the seasonal space heating energy efficiency of local space heaters includes a 
correction factor called F(4), which accounts for electric heater energy consumption in standby

2.2.3. Inadequate definitions concerning gas and liquid fuel local space heaters 
As explained in section 1.5.3 of the review study, some stakeholders have claimed that the terms 
used to define the different types of gas and liquid heaters are not aligned with the market. In this 
respect, the LSH Regulation differentiates between “open fronted” and “closed fronted” as being 
equivalent to open combustion and closed combustion respectively. But they are not the same. A 
heater can be closed fronted but feature open or closed combustion, depending on whether the air 
necessary for combustion is taken from the heated room or from the open air through a flue duct. In 
fact, the definition of open fronted given in the LSH Regulation has led some manufacturers to 
place on the market as open fronted, heaters that are in reality closed fronted/open combustion, 
whose energy efficiency requirements should be more stringent The inadequacy of the definitions 
results therefore in lost energy savings.  
2.2.4. Circular economy: insufficient availability of spare parts 
A 2016 study carried out for DG Environment29 shows that the availability and prices of spare parts 
are among the major barriers to reparability. Spare parts are usually not available or it takes a long 
time to deliver them, creating the incentive to get rid of the product and replace it instead of 
repairing it. Some producers deliver spare parts only to their partners, thus hindering competition 
among the latter and independent repairers.  
2.2.5. Circular economy: lack of rules on upgradability of controllers 
Used but well-functioning heaters may be unnecessarily replaced because their controllers and 
functions are not upgradeable. Redesigning products whose controllers can be replaced by more 
functional and efficient ones, would diminish the number of old devices that are scrapped and 
would also improve the energy efficiency of those old devices. No advances in the heater 
technology itself are expected, but future efficiency gains could be realised due to more efficient 
controllers, if it was possible to upgrade the controller. 
2.2.6. Circular economy: incomplete information on the end-of-life of appliances 
Analyses carried out during the review study show benefits of providing recyclers with sufficient 
and good quality information related to the recycling and disposal of heaters, in order to maximise 
material recovery and therefore reduce the costs of recycling.  
According to the review study, local space heaters are rather simple devices, and their material 
composition consists mostly of different types of metals. Metals are highly recyclable compared to 
other materials, which gives a high recycling rate for local space heaters, above 87%. In spite of

2.3. 
How likely is the problem to persist? 
Further energy savings from local space heaters do not depend on breakthrough technological 
improvements that would naturally replace the existing stock, but rather on reducing energy 
consumption by fitting heaters with suitable controllers that already exist in the market. In the 
absence of an amendment to the LSH Regulation, in-use energy savings will stagnate because out-
of-scope products will continue to be fitted with unsuitable controllers impeding further reduction 
of energy consumption.  
As far as circular economy, the repair activity of local space heaters is currently inexistent 
according to stakeholder information and there is no driver that could reverse this situation in the 
coming years.  
It can therefore be concluded that the problems will persist in the absence of any intervention. 
3. 
WHY SHOULD THE EU ACT? 
3.1. 
Legal basis 
Article 114 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union (TFEU) is the legal base for 
measures related to the functioning of the internal market and therefore constitutes the legal base 
for the Ecodesign Framework Directive30 and the derived legislation such as the LSH Regulation.  
In addition, the Ecodesign Framework Directive lays down a set of criteria to identify energy-
related products that may be subject to ecodesign measures. According to the review study, local 
space heaters are eligible on the basis of the potential energy savings that can still be achieved 
through the introduction of additional cost-effective measures.  
3.2. 
Subsidiarity: Necessity of EU action 
The LSH Regulation hinders the level playing field for manufacturers across the EU by creating a 
competitive disadvantage between those manufacturers that fulfil the ecodesign rules in force and 
those who do not. The good functioning of the internal market for local space heaters needs to be 
assured through EU action by modifying the scope of the LSH Regulation. Otherwise Member 
States could make different interpretations of that scope and could hinder the placing of specific 
products on their respective national markets on the basis of their diverging interpretations.  
Further, the contribution of ecodesign to the Green Deal objectives, in particular to the achievement 
of carbon neutrality by 2050 and the mobilisation of industry for a clean and circular economy, 
would be undermined if collective EU action is not taken on the efficiency of energy-related 
products such as local space heaters.

3.3. 
Subsidiarity: Added value of EU action 
Ecodesign measures contribute to the global EU energy and climate objectives by setting 
requirements that influence the characteristics of energy related products. In this respect, the 
approach from ecodesign requirements is different than the approach from other EU energy and 
climate legal acts such as EED, EPBD31 and RED32, which are based on the implementation by 
Member States of solutions based on existing technologies, but do not directly require the 
development of the technologies themselves. Direct EU action through ecodesign rules reinforce 
therefore the actions taken at national level. Ecodesign measures can only be addressed at EU level 
since they act directly on the product, where uniformity needs to be assured to avoid different 
national rules undermining the EU internal market for that product.  
In addition, the modification of the LSH Regulation can enhance the efficiency of MSAs. Ifthe 
scope of the LSH Regulation is clarified, MSA’s will spend less time selecting the products subject 
to inspection and will not need to discuss with industry the application of the ecodesign rules to 
specific product sub-groups.

As indicated in section 1.1, it is expected that the effect of the future ESPR on the baseline will be 
modest. First, the new products in scope of the ESPR do not consume energy during their use, and 
therefore the increase in energy in scope will primarily relate to embedded energy (manufacture, 
distribution and EoL of products), which is only 0,7% of the energy use during the lifecycle of local

develop and adopt secondary legislation and for it to come in force will mean that substantial 
impacts could not be expected for the next ten years. All these factors mean that any changes 
caused by the ESPR to the EU energy baseline for local space heaters can be expected to be 
minimal. Major differences from the BAU explained in section 5.2 will come from the current 
situation of high energy prices rather than from the new scope of the ESPR.

5.3.2. Policy option 2 (PO2): Clarifying and expanding the scope 
PO2 aims at fixing loopholes present in the LSH Regulation through the following measures: 
- 
PO2a. Removing the exemption for slave heaters, so that slave heaters come in the scope of the 
LSH Regulation. This measure will bring about energy savings by broadening the scope of the 
legislation and very importantly, by removing the legal loophole whereby heaters and the 
corresponding controllers are sold separately in order to sidestep the application of the LSH 
Regulation. For slave heaters and controllers sold separately, this measure must be 
complemented with the mandatory provision of information to easily identify heaters and 
controllers that can be installed together in order to comply with the ecodesign limits. Heaters 
should therefore include product information about which controllers can be fitted to the slave 
heater and which functions those controllers should feature in order to maximise energy 
efficiency and likewise, controllers should include information about the type of local space 
heater they are suitable for and their specific functions. This information should not only be 
included in the user’s manual but also be visibly displayed on the packaging of the product and 
as part of the product information provided on the Internet. 
- 
PO2b. Including towel heaters in the scope of the LSH Regulation: specific ecodesign limits 
will apply to towel heaters, which should be nevertheless different for those with heat output ≥ 
250 W and those with heat output < 250 W. Towel heaters with capacities ≥ 250 W are 
powerful enough to heat large bathrooms as the only heat source, hence stricter requirements 
should be set for these large heaters. For those below 250 W, as their capacity only allows for 
heating smaller and well-insulated bathrooms and the cost of controllers needed to fill the 
higher efficiency requirements could be too high compared to the current purchase cost of the 
heaters, more lenient efficiency requirements should be set.  
- 
PO2c. Including tube heaters between 120 and 300 kW in the scope of the LSH Regulation.  
- 
PO2d. Adapting the definitions for local space heaters using oil or gas: a modification of the 
definitions for open and closed-fronted local space heaters to better reflect the terminology used 
by the industry and in the standards, thus reducing the risk of misinterpretation of the LSH 
Regulation and ensuring that all the products inside the defined categories fulfil the ecodesign 
criteria. Furthermore, by defining three instead of two categories for gas and liquid fuel local 
space heaters, stricter and more fitting energy efficiency requirements could be set for each 
group and consequently reduce energy consumption.  
Stakeholders’ views 
Stakeholders generally support option PO2a, although manufacturers think that it is important to 
reduce the wording of the complementary information about heaters and controllers to avoid 
burdensome translations when exporting to different Member States. They also request the LSH 
Regulation to spell out the information to be displayed on the product. Producers of heating cables 
and mats expressed doubts about the benefit of mandating information on heaters sold without 
controllers, as they already do it in the absence of a legal obligation. A large company doubted the 
effectiveness of heater-controller combinations when they each come from a different 
manufacturer, and requested the legislation to mandate manufacturers to develop a complete 
solution to be placed on the market. Some stakeholders also suggested to adapt the scope of the 
LSH Regulation so that controllers are specifically included, while others suggest just to add a 
definition of controller. This is however not necessary because removing the exemption for slave 
heaters already ensures that the whole system (heater + controller) fulfils the ecodesign 
requirements, and because the calculation of the energy efficiency of a local space heater already 
includes the effect of controllers.

Stakeholders, including industry associations and individual companies, agree with PO2b, PO2c

less powerful than 250W are capable of heating small, well-insulated bathrooms. Some industry

output powers sufficient to heat a medium-sized room. The compromise generally accepted by 
stakeholders is to set a thresthold at 250W, with more lenient requirements for heaters below that

5.3.3. Policy option 3 (PO3): defining ecodesign limits for low power modes 
In order to ensure an energy efficient behaviour of local space heaters in low power modes, 
minimum values for standby (0,5W), off (0,2W), idle (0,1W) and network standby (2W) modes are 
included and local space heaters would need to comply with those values in order to be placed on 
the market. This is in line with other ecodesign regulations, like the washing machine regulation, 
where similar adaptations have been made. At the same time, the F(4) factor should be removed 
from the formula to calculate the seasonal energy efficiency of local space heaters because it 
becomes irrelevant.  
Stakeholders’ views 
No stakeholders oppose PO3. A large company has suggested setting a new F(4) applying to low 
power modes additional to standby, but a majority support removing F(4) and setting instead new 
minimum requirements for different additional low power modes which reflect more faithfully the 
real behaviour of local space heaters. 
5.3.4. Policy option 4 (PO4): circular economy 
- 
4a. Including requirements on the availability of spare parts and on repair and maintenance 
information 
Given the broad range of local space heaters and their different prices, the LSH Regulation could 
include different lists of spare parts to be availablefor each of the following product sub-groups:  
- portable heaters; 
- fixed heaters, towel heaters and underfloor heaters; 
- storage appliances; 
- electric visibly glowing radiant heaters; 
- gas and liquid fuelled heaters. 
The benefits of reparability for local space heaters need to be very carefully weighed. Section 6.7 of 
the review study already concluded that resource-saving requirements might be counterproductive, 
since: 
- 
Local space heaters lifetime l is already long for many sub-groups at around 15 years for 
electric heaters (excluding the cheaper portable devices that average 9 years) and 20 years for 
gas fuelled.  

<!-- page:25 -->
 
24 
- 
If spare parts have to be available for a long period, for instance 20 years or more, 
manufacturers may have to store spare parts that may never be used. 
- 
Consumers tend not to repair cheaper products, which are precisely the ones that feature lower 
lifetimes, of around 9 years for portable heaters. 
The lists of spare parts will therefore be limited to components whose replacement may be 
considered by the user on the basis of the costs involved and the age of the product when it breaks 
down. For safety reasons, the lists will also specify which parts should only be available for 
professional repairers and which ones could also be available for users.  
Due to the above restrictions, the lists of spare parts have been developed in close cooperation with 
APPLiA (Home Appliance Europe), the main representative of the sector.  
The listed spare parts should be available for 10 years (estimated average lifetime for the entire 
product group) after the production of the model stops and have a maximum delivery time of 15 
working days. Further, spare parts shall be easily removable with common available tools and 
without permanent damage to the appliance.  
In addition, repair and maintenance information shall be provided by suppliers to independent 
repairers.  
- 
4b. Including modularity requirements and setting a 10-year availability period of software 
updates for APPs 
Redesigning products so that controllers can be replaced by more functional and efficient ones, 
would diminish the number of old devices discarded and improve their energy efficiency. It would 
also reduce repair costs because users could change the controller themselves.  
In addition, in line with the update of controllers, any Apps that are linked to them should also be 
updated. Software update requirements can ensure that new versions of an App remain compatible 
with older products, thus ensuring a long lifetime. Software updates should therefore be available 
for 10 years after the production of a model stops. 
- 
4c. Including end-of-life information for improved disposal  
Information regarding dismantling the product and access to any of the components referred to in 
point 1 of Annex VII of the WEEE Directive37, must be available on a free access website. This 
information shall include the sequence of dismantling steps as well as the tools and technologies 
needed to remove the targeted components. The end-of-life information shall be available until at 
least 15 years after the placing on the market of the last unit of a product model. 
Stakeholders’ views 
Member States and NGOs support the availability of spare parts. Manufacturers did not oppose this 
but raised the following issues:

The delivery time for spare parts may be long due to the supply chain: consumers normally ask

- 
The lists of spare parts should inform about which parts are available for professional repairers

Most portable local space heaters are in the low-cost range (below 100 €). Repairing such

Furthermore, end users might replace their heater for many other reasons than the wish to get 
enhanced control functions, e.g. design, general house renovation, or installing central heating to 
replace local space heating. APPLiA points out that the costs of modularity can be very high and 
thus significantly increase the price of the product.  
Finally, Member States and NGOs’ supported requirements for software updates. Industry did not 
directly oppose this but opinions ranged from those stating that software updates are not a problem 
to those that were more cautious, since the communication is often programmed with proprietary 
protocols and, at some point in the lifetime of a heater, it becomes expensive to update a system if it 
is being phased out. 
5.4. 
Options discarded at an early stage 
5.4.1. Voluntary agreement by the industry 
According to the Ecodesign Framework Directive, voluntary agreements must be given priority 
provided they meet the objectives in a faster and more cost-effective manner than implementing 
acts. Further, Annex VIII of the Ecodesign Framework Directive sets out some indicative criteria 
that self-regulatory initiatives must incorporate to be alternative to an implementing measure. One 
of those criteria is representativeness, which means that industry and its associations taking part in a 
self-regulatory action must represent a large majority of the relevant economic sector. Main 
associations of the sector are APPLiA, EHI (European Heating Industry), HKI (Industrial 
Association of House, Heating and Kitchen Technology), AIE (European Association of Electrical 
Contractors), CEFACD (European Committee of Manufacturers of Domestic Heating and Cooking 
Appliances), EUHA (Electric Underfloor Heating Appliances) and ELVHIS (European Leading 
Association of Radiant Gas Heaters Manufacturers). The large amount of industry associations is 
the consequence of the heterogeneity of the sector in terms of product groups. None of them have 
shown willingness to undertake the leadership for a self-regulatory initiative in the different 
comments and position papers submitted about the amendment to the LSH Regulation. It is 
therefore not likely that a self-regulatory action would ever be operational.  
Further, the heterogeneity of the sector and the presence of a large number of SMEs would 
undermine the effectiveness of self-regulation. While an industry agreement would be suitable for 
sectors dominated by a small number of companies, or by companies located in a relatively reduced 

<!-- page:27 -->
 
26 
geographical area, an EU-wide agreement on local space heaters covering the totality of companies, 
many of them SMEs, would be complex to govern and would imply a high level of coordination 
between associations, national authorities and Commission equipped with the appropriate 
resources.  
On the basis of the foreseen complexity of implementation and lack of initiative by the private 
sector to undertake such initiative, a voluntary agreement has been ruled out.  
5.4.2. Mandatory third party certification 
The evaluation of the appropriateness to introduce third party certification for conformity 
assessment is a legal mandate set out in Article 7 of the LSH Regulation. This evaluation has been 
carried out in section 1.5.4 of the review study and is briefly explained here.  
The relevance of third party testing depends on the type of energy used by the heater. For electric 
heaters, third-party certification seems unnecessary and disproportionate in the view of the 
additional costs that it entails, because the energy efficiency tests do not leave room for 
interpretation and deviations for testing that can be misused by the manufacturer. For gas and liquid 
fuel heaters on the contrary, the large number of parameters to be adjusted (fuel quality, air flow 
and flue duct settings) makes it easier for the manufacturer to adjust the test in order to get the best 
possible result. Third party testing could thus be relevant for gas and liquid fuel heaters from a 
technical point of view. Nevertheless, the application of third party conformity assessment just for 
gas and liquid fuel heaters would entail the implementation of a burdensome procedure that would 
not apply to electric heaters, and would therefore undermine the level playing field between both 
types of devices by increasing the costs of non-electric heaters. Accreditation costs related to the 
services provided by certification authorities (laboratories) are estimated to be between 2.500 and 
3.000 €. Further, given the very low market share that gas and liquid fuel appliances represent, the 
benefits of third party certification in terms of energy and GHG emission savings would be 
minimal.  
Given the background above, third party certification has been ruled out. 
5.4.3. Modification of the verification tolerances 
The tolerances have been found to be in line with the accuracy of the current test methods and 
should therefore remain. 
5.4.4. Introduction of stricter ecodesign requirements and NOx emission limits 
The objective of both PO2 and PO3 is to reduce in-use energy consumption. An alternative to both 
policy options would have been to set stricter ecodesign limits by reducing the values of correction 
factors F(2) and F(3), which are applied to the formula used for the calculation of the energy 
efficiency from local space heaters. Section 7.1.2 of the review study discusses the possible 
introduction of stricter ecodesign measures making it more difficult to reach the minimum energy 
efficiencies set out in the legislation. Nevertheless, the review study concluded in section 7.2.2 that 
a reduction of the correction factors should only be made when there is enough evidence about how 
the current factors have performed. This was not the case when the review study was conducted 
because by that time the current limits had been in force for an insufficient period, to carry out the 
necessary assessment. Moreover, setting more demanding ecodesign requirements without first 
removing the LSH Regulation’s loopholes would have reduced the effectiveness of the measure, 
leading to wasted resources because the investments carried out by industry would not have been 
fully productive and the burden costs calculated in section 6.5 would have remained or even 

<!-- page:28 -->
 
27 
increased. It seemed therefore reasonable to focus on alternative measures that would not imply 
setting new limits. For the same reason, stricter pollutant emission requirements have also been 
discarded.  
A few NGO’s supported setting stricter energy efficiency and NOx emission requirements. The rest 
of stakeholders, including industry associations and individual companies, proposed to keep the 
current values and to assess the topic at the next review of the LSH Regulation.  
5.4.5. Removing the conversion coefficient (CC) from the calculations of the energy 
efficiency 
The CC is used to express final energy in terms of primary energy, which allows to compare heaters 
using electricity to heaters using oil or gas. It has been argued that the CC adds complexity to the 
calculation of the energy efficiency without really being representative of real efficiency, and 
therefore it has been considered to remove the CC and focus exclusively on the consumption of 
final energy. 
Even though it is always desirable to reduce the complexity of the LSH Regulation, removing the 
CC would make it impossible to compare electric heaters with oil and gas heaters, with a view to 
the future possible inclusion of electric heaters in the energy label for local space heaters (currently 
only fossil fuel heaters are in the scope of the energy label). Moreover, the CC is used in other EU 
regulations and is widely accepted. The decision on its removal is therefore outside of the scope of 
the revision of the LSH Regulation and, if adopted, should cover a number of regulations and not 
only local space heaters.

WHAT ARE THE IMPACTS OF THE POLICY OPTIONS? 
This section describes the impacts of each policy option from the environmental, economic and 
social points of view. Modelling principles follow the MEErP (Methodology for Ecodesign of 
Energy-related Products)38. The analytical model, described in detail in Annex 4, has been 
specifically developed for impact assessments by the external consultant Viegand Maagøe. The 
assumptions provided in Annex 4 have been systematically crosschecked with stakeholders, in 
particular with the industry. Future energy prices and financial rates follow the EU Reference 
Scenario 2020.   
It is necessary to note that the data used for the modelling of the different scenarios present some 
limitations, which are indicated here and explained in detail in Annex G of the review study and in 
Annexes 1 and 4 of this IA.  
First, there is a lack of field data about the energy performance of local space heaters. Annex G of 
the review study assumes that the efficiencies of all products in the scope of the LSH Regulation 
will not exceed the minimum requirements. This assumption has also been taken in this IA. There is 
no statistical information on products that perform better than needed and therefore the minimum 
threshold has been taken for the estimation of the impacts. This seems to be an adequate 
assumption because, as already pointed out, in the absence of technological improvements the in-
use energy savings per product unit are assumed to be rather modest.

basis of manufacturer turnover, by subtracting from the manufacturer turnover for a specific policy 
measure and year, the manufacturer turnover under BAU in the same year. This would imply the 
assumption that local space heater market develops as a perfectly competitive market where profits 
are zero. Although this is a simplification, it seems acceptable in the view of the high level of 
competition in the sector (around 60 manufacturers with 96% SMEs as indicated in section 5.3) and 
the low level of innovation, which tends to reduce product differentiation. 
The fifth and last important limitation is that PO4c impacts have not been quantified due to the lack 
of data on how the recycling process would improve as result of better information on 
recoverability and recyclability. The impact of PO4c will be anyway very small, first because local 
space heaters are nowadays highly recyclable, and second because embedded energy accounts only 
for 0,7% of the total energy use, as pointed out above. The measure is mostly intended to improve 
recoverability of electronic components, which will be increasingly present in the appliance sector 
as result of technological updates to the product.

6.2.1. Business turnover 
To calculate the business turnover it is assumed that the costs of adaptation of the product to the 
legislation will be fully passed on to consumers as higher product prices. Business turnover is 
obtained as follows: first, the 2020 prices per product subgroup estimated as explained in the 
introductory part of section 6, are entered in the price-efficiency elasticity formula explained in 
Annex 4, to obtain the evolution over time of the purchase cost per unit for each product subgroup. 
The elasticity formula makes purchase costs dependent on the energy efficiency of each product, as 
higher efficiencies will enable higher prices. The unitary price per year and product subgroup is 
then multiplied by the expected sales each year to get a total purchase cost per year and per product 
subgroup. Then the purchase costs for all subgroups are added in order to obtain a total purchase 
cost, which will be the turnover for the respective year. The last step is to multiply the turnover by a 
mark-up factor depending on whether the turnover corresponds to the manufacturer, the wholesale 
agent or the retailer. Mark-up factors are given in section 4.4 of Annex 4.

6.8. 
Impacts of current geopolitical instability 
In view of the sharply increasing energy prices due to the present political instability, the use of the 
energy prices from REF2020 for the projections raises the question of whether the analysis 
presented in the previous sections is realistic. The REPowerEU communication makes clear that 
there will be structural changes in EU energy supply that are likely to result in continuing higher 
energy prices. Higher energy prices would increase consumer expenditure during the use phase and 
therefore amplify the consumer savings from the proposed measures. The REPowerEU 
communication also underscores the need to step up energy efficiency investments in the light of 
the changed geopolitical situation. All these factors point to the likelihood that the assessment as 
described insufficiently values the energy savings from PO2 and PO3 and therefore gives too low 
an efficiency score to them. 
Section 8.4 includes an affordability analysis to understand how the considered options would be 
affected in a situation of escalating energy prices. In this analysis, the payback time for higher 
energy prices have been calculated assuming the adoption of ecodesign measures which are more 
ambitious than those assessed in this IA and that would provide an additional 2% increase in the 
energy efficiency of local space heaters (thus doubling the energy savings of the preferred policy 
option in this IA). In addition, Annex 8 includes a sensitivity analysis based on the Climate Target 
Plan MIX scenario46, with higher energy prices, lower carbon intensity and higher building 
renovation rates vis-a-vis REF2020. However, the prices from this scenario are lower than current 
prices. There is currently no PRIMES scenario that has been adapted to the new situation.

7.3. 
Coherence with other EU policies and legislative framework 
7.3.1. Coherence with the overarching objectives of EU policies 
From the analysis in section 6, it is clear that the proposed options aim to contribute to achieving 
Treaty objectives in relation to energy and the environment, and are fully consistent with EU policy 
goals such as set out in the Green Deal. In particular, the options contribute explicitly to the EU’s 
climate ambition for 2030 and 2050 and mobilising industry for a clean and circular economy as 
discussed in that Communication. The options have also been designed to ensure as much as 
possible a simplification of requirements in line with Better Regulation goals.   
7.3.2. Coherence with Article 15(5) of the Ecodesign Framework Directive 
Article 15(5) of the Ecodesign Framework Directive requires implementing measures to fulfil a 
number of criteria, against which all measures must be assessed. Only the criteria on “no significant 
negative impact on consumers in particular as regards affordability and life-cycle costs” is not 
fulfilled by some of the measures. In particular, PO2a and PO4b raise the price of the product by 
5,8% and 8% respectively, as shown in table 6-7. However, PO2a leads to offsetting energy savings 
in use that rapidly repay the additional purchase cost. In contrast, the additional costs of PO4b are 
not recovered in the lifetime period of a local space heater. Likewise, PO2d increases the price of 
the product and is not able to offset it with energy savings. However, this measure still yields 
environmental benefits, as shown in table 7-1. 
7.3.3. Coherence with the subsidiarity and proportionality principles 
Section 3 already assesses subsidiarity. All policy options have implications on the design and 
characteristics of the product and are therefore better addressed at EU than national level in order to 
ensure the correct functioning of the internal market.

8.2. 
REFIT (simplification and improved efficiency) 
The proposed policy option is in line with the REFIT objectives of simplification and reduction of 
red tape. The clarification and extension of the scope resulting from PO2 sets out a level playing 
field for industry and for MSAs, simplifying their work by laying down a clearer distinction 
between products in and out of the scope of the legislation. MSAs have reported to the Commission 
that confusing scope, in particular as regards of slave heaters, is the origin of loss of time for 
selection of the products to be inspected, discussions with manufacturers and additional red tape. 
With the planned modification MSAs will be able to spend more time on actual enforcement. In 
addition, by broadening the scope of the LSH Regulation there will be less risk that Member States 
take individual legislative action to regulate the range of products currently out of the scope and 
therefore prevents the rise of cost of compliance with different national rules.  
Additionally, as discussed in section 5.4.2, there is no need to include third party certification and 
the corresponding costs will be saved. 
8.3. 
Application of the ‘one in, one out’ approach  
There will be around 1,3 M€ administrative costs linked to the need to update the DoC’s for the 
products currently in the scope of the LSH Regulation and to issue a new DoC for the products that 
will come in the scope after the adoption of the measures. No potential cost savings in 
administrative activities performed by businesses were identified that could be included in the 
current revision of the legislative framework on local space heaters.  
8.4. 
Overview in the context of current energy prices 
Energy prices are sharply increasing due to the current situation of political instability. This strong 
increase, which was not foreseen when the review study was carried out, could have encouraged the 
introduction of additional ecodesign measures because higher energy costs would make those 
measures to be cost-efficient. For instance stricter ecodesign limits by reducing the correction 
factors F(2) and F(3), which is an option ruled out at an early stage as explained in section 5.4, 
could have been included among the policy options under assessment.  
It is not possible at this stage to make a fully-fledged impact assessment considering current energy 
prices because the available scenarios have not been updated with those prices. It is however 
possible to carry out an analysis of the impact on affordability (payback period) of introducing 
additional ecodesign measures, for different energy prices. Affordability is a key parameter for this 
analysis because other important impacts such as energy consumption reduction and GHG emission 
savings can only improve with the inclusion of additional ecodesign measures.

HOW WILL ACTUAL IMPACTS BE MONITORED AND EVALUATED? 
The main tool to verify compliance with ecodesign and energy labelling requirements is the market 
surveillance carried out by national authorities. Further, homogeneity of market surveillance actions 
at EU level is guaranteed by the Administrative Cooperation Groups, under which MSAs and the 
Commission meet several times per year with the objective to ensure efficient, comprehensive and 
consistent market surveillance across the Union. 
In addition, surveillance activities are supported by the Commission through EEPLIANT3, which is 
a Horizon 2020-funded project with a budget of €6,85 million49, involving a consortium of MSAs.

The project runs from June 2019 until November 2023 and includes a work package on heaters50, 
among them local space heaters. In the frame of EPPLIANT3, MSAs carry out market surveillance

authorities in their regular enforcement activities. Those activities are focused on document 
inspections, analysis of the content of web-shops, verification of EPREL data and testing of

On local space heaters, each MSA will have to screen at least 15 models and select at least 10

three product subgroups: gas-fuelled, biomass-fuelled and electric. Out of those 207, 143 models

ecodesign rules at lab facilities.  
The project is ongoing until mid-2023 and the results are not yet available, but it is expected that 
EPPLIANT3 is able to estimate the overall level of compliance of the products in the market with 
respect to the ecodesign rules. MSAs will need to take the EPPLIANT3 conclusions into account in 
order to optimise efforts by focussing on those specific issues where level of compliance is 
deficient. This will also apply to the enforcement of the upcoming TD Ecodesign and Energy 
Labelling Regulations.  
Finally, the revised TD Ecodesign and Energy Labelling Regulations will include a review clause 
that will mandate, inter alia, the evaluation of the effectiveness and regulatory efficiency and 
relevance of the revised regulations 5 years after their entry into force. The results of this 
evaluation, which will involve the acquisition of independent market data as well as extensive 
stakeholder consultation, should be presented to stakeholders and Member States in the Ecodesign 
and Energy Labelling Consultation Forums. In addition, the European Commission will be 
regularly analysing the latest updated data of the database EPREL, in order to monitor the evolution 
of the market as regards of energy efficiency, condensation efficiency, refrigerants used in the 
appliances and level of noise. This will allow the Commission to early detect possible technical 
improvements that can be assessed in future revisions.

ANNEX 4: ANALYTICAL METHODS 
This Annex describes in detail the data and assumptions the models are based on. 
1. 
SALES 
Current and past sales figures are based on data provided by GfK. The main difficulty for the 
development of the sales forecast has been that the sales figures provided by GfK, PRODCOM and 
other sources do not provide enough granularity as to fit the specific product categories. For this 
reason the study team combined the latest data from GfK (and to a lesser extend PRODCOM) with 
the distribution between various base cases in the previous preparatory study and impact 
assessment, which was then presented at the two stakeholder meetings held during the review 
study. Stakeholder inputs to this data were then collected and projections were adapted. This 
process was reiterated in the second stakeholder meeting, until no further comments were received. 
The main outcomes from this process were the following: 
- 
The sales of electric space heaters will be stable between 2016 and 2050. Different 
stakeholders do not agree among themselves, and some propose and increase while others 
suggest a decrease in future sales. Stable sales was the compromise most could agree to.  
- 
The sales numbers of domestic gas appliances estimated in the 2015 impact assessment turned 
out to be too high, since the market has been declining for years. Therefore the current sales of 
domestic gas appliances were corrected on the basis of the data from the two industry 
associations CEFACD and HHIC55, which together represent around 80 % of the total market. 
As a result, it has been estimated that the sales of domestic gas appliances will decrease 30 % 
towards 2030 and remain stable afterwards. 
- 
Regarding liquid fuel heaters, no stakeholders have supplied any information, but it is known 
that the sales of liquid fuel heaters are roughly 10 % of the sales of gas heaters, for which 
reason it has been assumed that the share will remain constant over the considered period.  
During the review process and dialogue with stakeholders, it also became apparent that the category 
“radiant heaters” (electric) did not represent any products on the market in the way it was defined in 
the LSH Regulation. This category was therefore left empty in all calculations, and removed from 
the definitions in the proposal for a legislative review.

TURNOVER AND EMPLOYMENT 
The modelling of the turnover has been calculated on the basis of the sales evolution and product 
price explained in sections 1 and 4.1 of this Annex respectively, and applying the mark-up factors 
in point 4.4 of this Annex.  
In addition, it has been estimated from stakeholder inputs that 60% of manufacturers of electric 
heaters are located inside EU (90-100% outside EU for entry level products, but 100% inside EU 
for mid-to-high level products), while 100% of manufacturers of gas and liquid heaters are located 
inside the EU . 
The employment has been determined by considering that its evolution is proportional to the 
business turnover, corrected negatively by an increase in labour productivity. For electric local 
space heaters, the correction has been drawn from Eurostat data for “Manufacture of electric 
domestic appliances” 56 between 2008 and 2014. Unfortunately, no data beyond 2014 are available. 
The graph below shows turnover versus number of employees, and it is seen that while turnover 
stays roughly stable in the period after the financial crisis, i.e. 2009-2014, employment dropped at a 
rate of 2,8%/year, reduction that is attributed to the improvement in production efficiency in the 
sector. Therefore the employment model used in this IA for the baseline has been calculated as the 
evolution of the turnover minus 2,8% points.

4.5. 
Use hours 
The purpose of the space to be heated has a strong influence on how the local space heaters are 
used in terms of temperature settings and use hours, the main differentiation being between service 
and residential purposes. Furthermore, the temperature settings and the number or hours of 
functioning of a local space heater are influenced by its use as either a primary or a secondary 
heating source.  
This study considers that for the service sector, gas and liquid fuelled (luminous and tube) heaters 
are used 8 hours a day at a temperature setting of 18 °C and 16 hours a day at a temperature setting 
of 14 °C, as they are usually the primary heat source in the places where they are installed58. 
As far as residential use, it is considered that heaters are used an average of 1,5 hours/day in the 
heating season when installed as secondary heat source, while when installed as primary heat 
source they are used an average of 8 hours at a setting of 20 °C and 16 hours at a setting of 15,5 °C, 
as recommended in standard EN 12831. 
The Heating degree days (HDD)59 are provided by Eurostat60 for the years 1990 to 2020. The 
projection until 2050 is carried out by a linear forecast in HDD development from 2020 to 2050, 
yielding a slightly decreasing (-0,00365 HHD/year) heat demand due to global warming.

ANNEX 6: ANALYSIS OF CURRENT ECODESIGN REQUIREMENTS 
FOR LOCAL SPACE HEATERS 
In the context of the Better Regulation policy65, the Commission is engaged to evaluate 
all EU activities intended to impact society or the economy. For the review of the 
Ecodesign Regulation on local space heaters, three out of the five standard evaluation 
criteria foreseen by Better Regulation need to be addressed, namely the effectiveness, 
efficiency and relevance of the proposed measures66.  
The other two evaluation criteria, coherence and EU added-value, have already been 
addressed at the framework level, i.e. in 2012, when the Ecodesign Directive was 
reviewed67. Later, a joint evaluation of the Ecodesign and Energy Labelling Directives68 
was carried out by the Commission in 2015, the main outcome being that the ecodesign 
and energy labelling measures in place are effective and bring tangible and substantial 
energy and cost savings. The implementation of the two Directives is estimated to save 
175 Mtoe primary energy per year by 2020, which corresponds to 19% savings with 
respect to business-as-usual energy use for those products. These policies will deliver 
almost half of the 20% energy efficiency target by 2020. Dependency on imports of 
energy would be reduced by 23% and 37% for natural gas and coal, respectively. In total, 
the ecodesign and energy labelling measures in place to date are estimated to have saved 
end-users of products 100 billion euro per year in 2020 through lower utility bills 
(translated into roughly 500 euros yearly savings in each household). 
This annex presents the relevant findings of the review of the current LSH Regulation 
focussing on its effectiveness, efficiency and relevance. The assessment builds on the 
information collected during the 2019 review study and any data updates on development 
collected during the present impact assessment. 
1.

The impacts on energy consumption (and subsequently GHG emissions, which are 
directly dependent on energy) and NOx reflect the effectiveness of the LSH Regulation, 
namely its capacity to achieve the objectives. The period analysed is 2000-2020.

ANNEX 7: SOME MEASURES THAT DO NOT ENTAIL ANY DIRECT 
ENVIRONMENTAL OR ECONOMIC IMPACT 
The amendment to the LSH Regulation will incorporate some modifications that do not 
entail any environmental or economic impact and have therefore not been included in the 
policy options. Nevertheless, it is convenient to mention them in a separate annex. Those 
modifications are the following: 
1. 
REPHRASING THE OVERALL DEFINITION OF LOCAL SPACE HEATERS 
During the review study, multiple stakeholders made different suggestions for a new 
definition of local space heaters in order to be able to be more precise about the type of 
product included in that definition. Some suggested removing the term “human thermal 
comfort” as this wording could create a loophole as regards of decorative heaters or 
towel heaters. However, removing those words would raise doubts about the inclusion in 
the scope of other devices such as those used in e.g. agriculture, industrial facilities and 
other sectors, which should not be affected by the LSH Regulation. It has been agreed 
nevertheless to replace the term “reach and maintain a certain level of human thermal 
comfort” by “contribute to a certain level of human thermal comfort”, to make clear that 
products that are not the only source of heat are also affected. In addition, the definition 
has been further simplified by removing redundancies such as the term “in combination 
with heat transfer to a fluid” which is equivalent to “combined with a heat output to other 
spaces”. The sentence “through use of the Joule effect or combustion” is unnecessary and 
has also been removed.  
With all the above, the definition of local space heater will finally be simplified as 
follows: 
Current definition: local space heater means a space heating device that emits heat by 
direct heat transfer or by direct heat transfer in combination with heat transfer to a fluid, 
in order to reach and maintain a certain level of human thermal comfort within an 
enclosed space in which the product is situated, possibly combined with a heat output to 
other spaces and is equipped with one or more heat generators that convert electricity or 
gaseous, liquid or solid fuels directly into heat, through use of the Joule effect or 
combustion of fuels respectively. 
New definition: local space heater means a device that is equipped with one or more heat 
generators with the purpose of converting electricity or gaseous or liquid fuels directly 
into heat to contribute to a certain level of human thermal comfort in the enclosed space 
in which it is situated by direct heat transfer, possibly combined with a heat output to 
other spaces or with heat transfer to a fluid. 
Stakeholder views: stakeholders are in favour of the suggested changes and especially the 
wording ‘contribute’ instead of ‘reach and maintain’.  
2. 
CHANGING THE DEFINITION OF AIR-HEATING APPLIANCES 
It is suggested to change the definition of air-heating appliances as the definition of the 
same product should always be aligned across different regulations to avoid confusion. In 

<!-- page:85 -->
 
84 
 
the current definition, it is not clear what is meant with the words “only to an air-based 
heating system; or that can only be ducted”. Hence, it is suggested to incorporate the 
definition from Regulation 2016/228174 as follows: 
Current definition: air heating product means a product providing heat to an air-based 
heating system only that can be ducted and is designed to be used while fastened or 
secured in a specific location or wall mounted which distributes the air by means of an 
air moving device in order to reach and maintain a certain level of human thermal 
comfort within an enclosed space in which the product is situated. 
New definition, aligned with Regulation 2016/2281: air heating product means a device 
that incorporates or provides heat to an air-based heating system; is equipped with one or

air directly into the heated space by means of an air-moving device. A heat generator 
designed for an air heating product and an air heating product housing designed to be 
equipped with such a heat generator shall, together, be considered as an air heating 
product. 
Stakeholder views: No stakeholders opposed to the new definition.  
3. 
CHANGE THE DEFINITION OF “ELECTRIC PORTABLE HEATERS”  
A new definition for portable electric heaters shall ensure that this definition is restricted 
to heaters which can be only used in a portable mode, and which have no option to be 
used as fixed heaters. Hence, if any accessories are made available for fixing the heater, 
either together or separately from it, the heater will have to comply with the requirements 
for fixed heaters. 
Current definition: electric portable local space heater means an electric local space 
heater which is not an electric fixed local space heater, electric storage local space heater, 
electric underfloor local space heater, electric radiant local space heater, electric visibly 
glowing local space heater or slave heater. 
New proposed definition: electric portable local space heater means an electric local 
space heater that is designed to be portable in order to be operated in different rooms or 
locations. A portable appliance with features which can be used to fix it to a wall and/or 
floor is considered an electric fixed local space heater. 
Stakeholder views: No stakeholders opposed to the new definition.

IDENTIFICATION OF THE AFFECTED BUSINESSES 
In the absence of specific data on the number of manufacturers of local space heaters, 
including SMEs, the share of SMEs in the sector has been worked out from data from the 
Eurostat Structural Business Statistics. Eurostat provides turnover and number of SMEs 
for the entire appliance sector (NACE 275). The share of SMEs that are manufacturers of 
local space heaters is calculated by multiplying the number of SMEs for the appliance

measures. Assuming that the share SMEs/total companies remains the same for local 
space heaters as for the entire appliance sector, 57 SMEs would be equivalent to 96% of 
the manufacturers.  
2. 
THRESHOLD QUESTION: TO WHAT EXTENT IS THE INITIATIVE RELEVANT FOR 
SMES? 
Although SMEs are in the scope of the initiative, the latter is not specifically targeting 
SMEs. The initiative will apply to the whole sector of local space heaters, with no 
distinction between big companies and SMEs.

negative impacts on the companies affected. SMEs will not be negatively affected either

the LSH Regulation. Second, measure PO4b on modularity has also been ruled out

ANNEX 10: MULTI-CRITERIA ANALYSIS 
1. 
MULTI-CRITERIA DECISION ANALYSIS METHODOLOGY: BASIC DESCRIPTION OF 
SOCIAL MULTI-CRITERIA EVALUATION (SMCE) METHODOLOGICAL FRAMEWORK 
AND SOFTWARE TOOL 
This section describes the analysis of the impacts carried out with the software 
SOCRATES (SOcial multi CRiteria AssessmenT of European policieS) developed by 
the Joint Research Centre. SOCRATES has been used to validate the choice of the 
preferred policy option according to a mathematical model.  
SOCRATES is a new multiple criteria assessment software tool, explicitly designed for ex-ante 
Impact Assessment (IA) problems76.  
Quantitative evidence plays an important role in many IAs, but also qualitative data such 
as stakeholder input, conclusions of evaluations, as well as scientific and expert advice 
are frequently used. This generates a multitude of criteria, which should be consistently 
integrated and evaluated when comparing policy options. The most widespread 
multidimensional approach to ex-ante IAs is multi-criteria decision analysis, which forms 
the basis for social multi-criteria evaluation (SMCE77), which has been explicitly 
designed for public policy. SMCE allows taking into account a wide range of assessment 
criteria, such as the impact on SMEs, the degree of protection of fundamental rights, 
consumer protection, etc. while all the multidimensional profiles of the problem remain 
in their original scales of measurement. Indeed, the latter is the main difference with 
traditional cost-benefit analysis (CBA), which grounds on steps like monetizing all 
social, environmental, and human rights aspects. In this respect, CBA and SMCE are not 
conflictual but complementary, as CBA can be utilised as component of a SMCE 
framework, dealing with the economic dimension.   
Overall, the objective of SOCRATES and the underlying SMCE methodology is not to 
substitute policy-makers through a mathematical model, but to improve their 
understanding of the main features of the problem at hand, such as key assumptions, 
degree of uncertainty, robustness of results and overall technical and social defensibility 
of options chosen. While SMCE has already been applied in a multitude of policy 
problems, its recent technical implementation through SOCRATES is now applied for 
this Impact Assessment.   
SMCE proceeds on the basis of following main concepts: dimensions, objectives, 
criteria, weights, criterion scores, impact matrix and compromise solution.

• Dimension is the highest hierarchical level of analysis and indicates the scope of 
objectives, criteria and criterion scores.  
• Objectives indicate the direction of change desired, e.g. growth has to be maximized, 
social exclusion has to be minimized, and carbon dioxide emissions have to be 
reduced.  
• A criterion is a function that associates alternative actions with a variable indicating 
its desirability.  
• Weights are often used to represent the relative importance attached to dimensions, 
objectives and criteria. The idea behind this practice is very intuitive and easy, that is, 
to place the greatest number in the position corresponding to the most important 
factor.  
• A criterion score is an assessment of the impact consistent with a given criterion 
with reference to a policy option. Criterion scores can be both qualitative and 
quantitative.  
• The impact matrix presents in a structured way, the information on the various 
criterion scores, i.e. each element of the matrix represents the performance of each 
option according to each criterion.  
In general, in a multi-criterion problem, there is no solution (ideal or utopia solution) 
optimizing all the criteria at the same time, and therefore “compromise solutions” have 
to be found. 
In summary a SMCE approach can supply a methodological framework where the 
hierarchical structure of the option comparison step of a typical ex-ante IA (including 
dimensions, objectives and evaluation criteria) is clarified as much as possible by means 
of well-established concepts in the decision theory literature. This might help in 
increasing the degree of homogeneity across IA studies. The SOCRATES software helps 
structuring such a methodological framework.  
A typical SOCRATES input requires the definition of policy options (called alternatives) 
dimensions, objectives and criteria. This information leads to the construction of an 
impact matrix, which may include crisp, stochastic or fuzzy measurements of the 
performance of an alternative with respect to an evaluation criterion. Qualitative 
information can be introduced too (in the form of linguistic or ordinal criterion scores). 
Weights as importance coefficients, may also be introduced. They can be attached to 
dimensions or criteria. Indifference and preference thresholds can also be introduced 
when needed. Generally a social conflict matrix is also constructed, where the impacts of 
each policy option on each social group are presented in a transparent way. 
In this IA, for better granularity the analysis is made with respect to the individual 
measures instead of for each of the policy options as a whole, so that it is possible to 
combine those individual measures in a single and preferred policy option. The time 
horizon taken for the analysis is 2050 because the impact of the measures related to 
circular economy mostly take place when the stock has aged, being therefore 2030 a too 
short term to assess PO4, in particular for PO4a. The rest of the policy options keep a 
similar behaviour throughout the whole period under study.

The importance of mathematical approaches in SMCE is their ability to allow a 
consistent aggregation of the diverse information. Otherwise, even if everybody would 

<!-- page:98 -->
 
97 
 
agree on the multidimensional nature of an IA study, the implementation in a real-world 
assessment exercise would be impossible. The standard objection might be that the 
aggregation of apples and oranges is impossible. Multi-criteria mathematics does answer 
to this objection in a definitive way. When using mathematical rules, consistency 
between the problem structuring and the ranking of policy options is guaranteed, this 
makes the overall IA study much more defensible. 
SOCRATES makes all required computations very quick. From a mathematical point of 
view, the information contained in the impact matrix useful for solving the so-called 
multi-criterion problem is: 
• 
Intensity of preference (when quantitative criterion scores are present). 
• 
Number of criteria in favour of a given alternative. 
• 
Weight attached to each single criterion. 
• 
Relationship of each single alternative with all the other alternatives. 
Combinations of this information generate different aggregation conventions, i.e. 
manipulation rules of the available information to arrive at a preference structure. The 
aggregation of several criteria implies taking a position on the fundamental issue of 
compensability. For example, in evaluating a policy option that presents a very bad 
environmental impact and a very good economic impact, it is clear that allowing or not 
for compensability and to which degree is the key assumption.  
An aggregation rule that is simple, non-compensatory and minimises the rank reversal 
phenomena is the Kemeny rule. Its basic idea is that the maximum likelihood ranking of 
policy options is the ranking supported by the maximum number of criteria (or criterion 
weights) for each pair-wise comparison, summed over all pairs of options considered. 
There is agreement in the literature that the Kemeny method is “the correct method” for 
ranking options, and that the only drawback of this aggregation method is the difficulty 
in computing it when the number of options grows. A numerical algorithm solving this 
computational drawback in an efficient way has been developed recently at JRC and has 
been implemented in SOCRATES78. 
Various authors have argued that the presence of qualitative information in evaluation 
problems concerning socio-economic issues is a rule, rather than an exception. Thus 
there is a clear need for methods that are able to take into account information of a 
"mixed" type (both qualitative and quantitative criterion scores). Moreover, ideally, this 
information should be precise, certain, exhaustive and unequivocal. Nevertheless, in 
reality, it is often necessary to use information which does not have those characteristics 
so that one has to face the uncertainty of a stochastic and/or fuzzy nature present in the 
data. Therefore, multi-criteria methods able to tackle consistently the widest types of 
mixed information should be considered as desirable ones in the IA framework.

Overall, the objective of SOCRATES is NOT substitution of policy-makers through a 
mathematical model, on the contrary, the objective is to improve their understanding of 
the main features of the problem at hand, such as key assumptions, degree of uncertainty, 
robustness of results and overall technical and social defensibility of options chosen. The 
philosopher Socrates said: “I cannot teach anybody anything. I can only make them 
think.” This is the main inspiring principle of the SOCRATES software too. 
The SOCRATES software offers a measurement framework where the various criterion 
scores can assess impacts by using both quantitative (e.g. as result of simulation models) 
and qualitative (e.g. results of participatory techniques) information, and the 
mathematical aggregation rule guarantees consistency and transparency of results. 
Three main components constitute the core of SOCRATES: multi-criteria, equity and 
sensitivity analyses. Multi-criteria analysis requires the definition of relevant dimensions, 
objectives and criteria. It uses weights as importance coefficients and clarify their role in 
the hierarchical structure. The impact matrix may include quantitative (including also 
stochastic and/or fuzzy uncertainty) and qualitative (ordinal and/or linguistic) 
measurements of the performance of an alternative with respect to an evaluation 
criterion. It supplies a ranking of the alternatives according to the set of evaluation 
criteria (i.e. the technical compromise solution/s).

ANNEX 11: THE ECODESIGN 
AND ENERGY LABELLING 
FRAMEWORK 
The Ecodesign Framework Directive and Energy Labelling Framework Regulation are 
framework rules, establishing conditions for laying down product-specific requirements 
in regulations adopted by the Commission. The Commission's role in the implementation 
of delegated and implementing acts is to ensure a maximum of transparency and 
stakeholder participation in presenting a proposal, based on generally accepted data and 
information, to the European Parliament and Council for scrutiny. Figure gives an 
overview of the procedures leading to a voluntary agreement, Ecodesign requirements or 
an Energy label. 
 
Figure A11-1. Overview of the three processes 
Ecodesign and Energy labelling are complementary. Ecodesign implementing measures 
lay down minimum energy efficiency requirements for a product group and therefore ban 
worse performing products. In contrast, energy labelling provides objective comparable 
information to enable consumers to choose better performing products through 
mandatory rating. In view of the complementarity, energy labelling delegated acts are 
usually adopted in parallel with Ecodesign implementing acts to ensure coherence 
between the measures. 
The process 
The process starts with establishing the priorities for Union action in this area. Priority 
product groups are selected based on their potential for cost-effective reduction of energy 
use and greenhouse gas emissions and follows a transparent process culminating in a 
working plan that outlines the priorities for the development of implementing measures 
for the next years. Working plans are adopted by the Commission after consultation of 
the Ecodesign and Energy labelling Consultation Forum. The forum brings together 
representatives of Member States and relevant stakeholders. 

<!-- page:110 -->
 
109 
 
The Ecodesign Working Plan 2016-2019 identified Local Space Heaters as a priority. A 
new Ecodesign and Energy labelling Working plan 2022-24 has now been adopted and 
retains this product.  
Once the product group has been selected, a preparatory study is undertaken by an 
independent consultant, also involving extensive technical discussions with interested 
stakeholders. The preparatory study follows the standard Methodology for the Ecodesign 
of Energy Related Products (MEErP). Subsequently, the Commission's first drafts of 
Ecodesign and energy labelling measures are submitted for discussion to the 
Consultation Forum. 
After the Consultation Forum, the Commission drafts an impact assessment, which after 
RSB approval accompanies the draft implementing measures in the Commission’s inter-
service consultation. In this and subsequent steps, the Parliament's functional mailboxes 
for delegated/implementing acts are copied on each message from the Commission 
services. After the inter-service consultation, stakeholders are alerted when the draft 
measures are published in the WTO notification database. 
After the WTO notification phase is completed, the two procedures follow different 
paths. The draft energy labelling delegated act is discussed in a Member States Expert 
Group where opinion(s) are expressed and consensus is sought but no vote is taken. The 
draft Ecodesign measure is submitted for vote to the Regulatory Committee. 
The European Parliament and Council have the right of scrutiny for a period of up to four 
months. Within this time, the co-legislators can block the adoption process by the 
Commission. Parliament committees sometimes discuss draft objections to measures 
(light bulbs and fridges in 2009) or vote to reject a measure (vacuum cleaners in 201380). 
On one occasion, an objection was adopted in plenary, blocking the measure for 
televisions in 200981.  
Today, 29 Ecodesign Regulations, 14 Energy Labelling Regulations, 3 voluntary 
agreements and 2 tyre labelling regulations have been implemented. An overview of the 
measures in force in March 2022 is available on the Europa website. 
Market Surveillance 
The Market Surveillance Regulation82 establishes the rules on Union market surveillance 
and control of products entering the Union market. Market Surveillance Authorities 
(MSAs), designated by Member States, will verify the conformity of the products with 
the requirements laid down in the implementing measures and delegated acts. Checks can 
be done on the product itself or by verifying the technical documentation.
